# The Clarity Doctrine: plain first, every post, every platform

Load-bearing for EVERY content artifact Tangent makes: carousels, single images, reels,
Shorts, stories, captions, titles, thumbnails, long-form packaging. That includes pieces made
outside the pipeline (a festive reel, an article announcement, anything Sparsh asks for
directly). CLAUDE.md non-negotiable #10 makes this file constitutional. The evidence base is
`research/clarity-2026-11/` (synthesis: `SYNTHESIS.md`).

## Why this exists

In November 2026 a 6-slide Panditji carousel went up and came down. Sparsh's wife read it and
said not one slide made sense. Every headline had been written to sound stylish:

| What we posted | What a stranger had to work out |
|---|---|
| "The AI kept moving Saturn. So I took the maths away from it." | Moving it how? Which maths? Away from what? |
| "One sky, two ways to count it." | Which two ways? Counting what? |
| "Checked against the sky's reference books." | Checked by whom? What books? What happened? |
| "427 ways to ask the wrong thing. Tested." | 427 what? Tested by whom, with what result? |
| "The phone does the maths. The model only explains it." | A fashion model? Explains what? |

Each line hides a fact that only the writer knows. The reader has to decode it, and on a feed
nobody decodes. They scroll. Enough posts like that and they unfollow.

Her recorded feedback (`research/clarity-2026-11/reader_feedback.md`) added two more failures.
Even the plainer lines on the earlier launch carousel ("It reads your day, in plain words.")
felt "very presentation-ish", as if no person were talking. And the cover set a mood instead
of saying what the post was about.

So there are three failures to guard against, each separate from the others:
1. **The riddle voice:** lines that hide the meaning.
2. **The deck voice:** clear lines with nobody talking.
3. **No single message:** many topics, and a cover that is not a label.

## The principle

**People on Instagram and YouTube want the point instantly. Make every line understandable in
one glance by someone who knows nothing about us, and make it sound like a person talking to
them. Style lives in the look, the photograph and the warmth of the voice. It never lives in
wordplay that hides the meaning.**

## The shape of every post: one sentence, unfolded in layers

A reader can stop at any layer and still leave with the point:

| Layer | What it carries | Test |
|---|---|---|
| 0. The message | One plain sentence a viewer would repeat to a friend | Can you write it plainly? |
| 1. The cover / first frame | What this post is about, in a few simple words: a label plus a stake, never a mood or a riddle | Cover-only read: a stranger can say what the post is about |
| 2. The headlines | One per slide, each the slide's whole point | Headline-only read: the headlines alone, in order, tell the story |
| 3. The body | Optional depth under each headline, for the interested | A skimmer who skips it loses nothing essential |
| 4. The caption | Line 1 restates the message; later lines carry the "more" layer | First ~125 characters make sense alone |
| 5. The depth path | Article, long video, link in bio | B knows where to go |

Dense slides are allowed when one plain headline leads them. The first-time reader put it
this way: "if I'm not interested, I can scroll past, but I still get the message". Any
intrigue on the cover must sit inside a plain statement ("I built an astrology app where the
AI isn't allowed to guess"), never in a teaser that withholds what the post is about.

The evidence, in short (details and sources in `research/clarity-2026-11/`):
- Needlessly complex writing makes the writer seem LESS intelligent, and the effect runs
  through how hard the text is to process (Oppenheimer 2006). Easy text is liked and trusted
  more (Reber, Schwarz and Winkielman 2004; Alter and Oppenheimer 2009). [RESEARCH-BACKED]
- People hold about 4 things in mind at once (Cowan 2010). Irrelevant words and decoding work
  steal that room (Mayer's coherence principle, d about 0.7). [RESEARCH-BACKED]
- Concrete, picturable words are processed and remembered better than abstract ones (Paivio's
  dual coding). [RESEARCH-BACKED]
- Experts also prefer plain, scannable text (NN/g 2017). Plain costs us nothing with the tech
  crowd. [INDUSTRY usability research]
- Instagram ranks on watch time, likes per reach and sends per reach; sends matter most for
  reaching new people (Mosseri, Jan 2025). Nobody sends a post they did not understand.
  [OFFICIAL-PLATFORM]
- In the 2026-11 harvest of about 100 top posts and videos, the strongest headlines were
  literal sentences saying who did what (6-15 words), and the weakest posts in the same feeds
  opened with an abstract idea, a metaphor or a stack of numbers. [CORPUS:
  research/clarity-2026-11/harvest_*.md]

Reading-grade formulas do NOT catch this. The failed carousel scores grade 5.0 (short words).
Short words can still hide the meaning. The test that matters is whether a stranger can say
back what the post means (the cold read, below).

## Our two audiences

Every artifact names its primary audience before anything is written.

- **A. The AI-curious everyday viewer.** Little interest in HOW AI works. Gets excited about
  the cool things AI can do, or that someone built with it. Gives a post about 2 seconds.
  Often reads English as a second language (a large part of our early audience is Indian):
  aim for grade 5-7 and short sentences.
- **B. The tech enthusiast.** Will tap "more", "link in bio" or the long video if a simple
  post makes them curious. Still skims. Still prefers plain words. Wants a path to depth, not
  depth on the slide.

**Two-layer design.** Write the slides so A gets the whole point. Put B's hook at the end or
in the caption ("How I stopped the AI from guessing: link in bio"). Depth (methods, numbers,
tests, code) lives off-feed: the article, the long video, the caption's later lines. Never put
a technical term on a slide "because enthusiasts like it". [RESEARCH-BACKED inverted pyramid +
NN/g; CORPUS: Axios, Kurzgesagt, @openai builder carousels]

`knowledge/audience/personas.md` maps our older, finer personas onto A and B.

## The Message Brief (mandatory, before any copy is written)

Fill `templates/message-brief.md` for every artifact. It has six parts:

0. **What kind of post this is, and its one job.** A story, a tip, an explainer, an
   announcement, a before-and-after. Pick ONE. A post that is an app pitch, a build story, an
   AI lesson and an article plug at once has no clear point. That was the second Panditji
   carousel's failure: every slide was clear, and the post as a whole wasn't.
1. **The one-line message.** Strictly ONE statement, the sentence a viewer would say to a
   friend afterwards. Plain words, about 12 words at most, no metaphor, no jargon, and no
   "and/so/where" chaining of two ideas into one sentence. Too complex: "I built a free
   astrology app where the AI can't make up where the planets are." One statement: "My
   astrology app doesn't let AI do the maths."
2. **Primary audience: A, B, or both (two-layer).** And why they would care, in their words:
   "So my horoscope is actually calculated, not made up."
3. **The arc: 3-5 beats.** One short plain sentence per beat. Read in order, the beats alone
   must tell the whole story to a 12-year-old: someone, a problem, a turn, a result. Each beat
   follows from the one before ("then", "because", "so", "now"). Pick a shape from the arc
   library below. **Then do the story check (process step 2) before writing any copy.**
4. **The picture for each beat.** What the viewer SEES. Plan the visual before the line
   (Cleo Abram's method). A beat with nothing to show is a beat to cut or rethink.
5. **The depth path.** Where B goes for more (article, long video, link in bio) and the one
   line that points there. Or "none".
6. **The voice and tone.** Who is talking and to whom ("Sparsh, to a friend who has never
   heard of the app"), and the tone: person-direct, person-warm, or person-witty (see Voice
   below). Log the tone so the tone experiment in `strategy/BETS.md` gets data.

If you cannot write the one-line message plainly, the post is not ready. Do not start
designing.

## The arc library

Pick one. Each beat is one slide (carousel) or one stretch of 5-15 seconds (reel). Beats are
written as plain sentences. Adapt, but keep the order: something the viewer already
understands comes first, then exactly one new idea, then the proof, then the next step.
This is "pace, then lead" from the NLP research, restated as a writing rule.

**1. I made a thing** (apps and builds). Sources: @openai's "This legend built an app to help
your grandparents not get scammed", a small builder's "Hi, I'm Jacklyn. I built Shelf by JC
with AI.", Luis P's numbered apps, and Riley Brown's reels.
- Cover: the person plus what was built plus what it does, said literally. "I built an app
  that tells you the good hours to start something. With AI." Show the app or Sparsh.
- Before: who I am and what I can't do ("I'm not an app developer").
- Then: the everyday problem I ran into, in a sentence a stranger could repeat.
- So: what I built and how, one plain sentence, with a real screenshot or prompt.
- Now: what it does for you today, with one number if it is real.
- Next step: try it / link in bio, or a question.

Chain the headlines Before / Then / So / Now (or Step 1 to N). Put a real screenshot on every
slide. Add one candid aside. Never lead with the tool or model name.

**2. Before and after** (AI-edited photographs)
- Cover: the finished photograph, with a plain label of what this is ("I let Claude edit my
  wedding photos. Here's the raw and the result.").
- The before: the raw camera shot.
- What changed: 2-3 changes in plain words, each as a short label on the picture ("Brighter
  faces", "Warmer sky").
- Who decided: Claude made the edits, Sparsh picked the one that ships.
- Optional: the next one / the gallery.

Never post a text-free gallery whose caption does not say what the images are. In the corpus
that got 7 likes. A text-free photo post works only when caption line 1 states the claim.

**3. Explain one thing** (AI explained simply; Harper Carroll's "Why AI forgets things")
- Beat 1, the question a normal person already asks, in their words.
- Beat 2, the answer in one plain line.
- Beat 3, one picture or everyday comparison that makes it visible.
- Beat 4, what it means for you.
- Beat 5, go deeper (link in bio).

**4. What went wrong, and the fix** (behind-the-scenes; enthusiast-leaning, two-layer)
- Beat 1, the concrete scene: "I asked the AI where Saturn was on my birthday. It gave a
  different answer every time."
- Beat 2, why, in one plain reason.
- Beat 3, the fix, in one plain sentence.
- Beat 4, proof it works: one check and its result, with what the number counts.
- Beat 5, the full story: link in bio.

**5. Guess** (a number story; Cleo Abram's "Guess how far the sun is")
- Beat 1, ask the viewer to guess something concrete.
- Beat 2-3, a ladder of plain steps toward the answer.
- Beat 4, the reveal plus one picture that makes the size visible.
- Beat 5, a tease for the next post.

**6. News and what it means for you** (timely)
- Beat 1, what happened, as a headline sentence (who did what).
- Beat 2, what it means for a normal person.
- Beat 3, one example.
- Beat 4, Sparsh's honest take or test.

## Line rules (every slide, frame, title and caption line)

1. **Say who did what to what.** A named subject, a plain verb, a concrete object.
   "I asked an AI where Saturn was." Not "The AI kept moving Saturn."
2. **No "the X" for something the reader has not met.** "The maths", "the model", "the
   sky's reference books" all assume shared knowledge. Introduce it first ("a small program"),
   then say "the program".
3. **Picturable nouns and filmable verbs.** Phone, birthday, photo, chart, sky, grandmother.
   Not workflow, pipeline, system, solution, logic, model. Verbs a camera could record:
   asked, built, edited, matched, blocked. Not enabled, optimized, leveraged, handled. In the
   2026-11 corpus audit, strong lines used a filmable verb 52% of the time against 7% for weak
   lines, and a picturable subject 74% against 33% (`research/clarity-2026-11/nlp_research.md`
   Part 3).
4. **No agentless fragments.** "Tested." "Checked." "Shipped." Write who did it and what
   happened: "I tested 427 tricky questions. It blocked every harmful one."
5. **Every number needs its noun and its result.** "427 ways" is decoration. "I tried 427
   tricky questions" is information. Two or three numbers per post, at most; one per slide.
6. **Jargon: define it in place in under 10 words, show it, or cut it.** NASA's "one
   light-day (16 billion miles)". "Agents are digital assistants that do tasks for you."
   For audience A, prefer cutting.
7. **Metaphor only when it is a picture of size or mechanism, from everyday life, and the
   plain claim sits in the same view.** Cleo's basketball-and-pin sun works. "One sky, two
   ways to count it" does not. Never a metaphor about ideas. One per post, at most.
8. **One idea per slide. One fact per sentence.** Sentences about 15 words or fewer
   (spoken: breath-length).
9. **Write to a person.** "You" and "I". Conversational English, the way Sparsh would say it
   to a friend over chai.
10. **Plain connectors.** So, but, then, now, step one, number two. No lyrical bridges.
11. **Honest limits.** Say what the AI does and does not do. On Panditji, say what is
    calculated and what is tradition.

**Banned shapes** (they feel like insight and hide the meaning; `tools/clarity_lint.py`
catches several):
- Aphorism pairs and parallel riddles: "One sky, two ways to count it." "The phone does the
  maths. The model only explains it."
- "It's not X, it's Y." "Not just X, but Y."
- Fragment payoffs: "Tested." "Checked." "Finally."
- A metaphor standing in for a real noun: "the sky's reference books" for "NASA's planet
  tables".
- Drumroll filler: "Here's the thing", "Let that sink in", "Read that again".
- A topic label instead of a claim ("Artificial Super Intelligence", "The fix").
- Clever coinages a stranger has never heard ("tokenmaxxing").

**When is a clever line allowed?** Only when it passes the one-glance test: it rests on a
phrase everyone already knows, AND the plain meaning is in the same view (the picture or the
next line decodes it). Proverb-style lines ("Take the long way") can stay vague and still work,
but only when all three of these hold:
- the reader fills the gap from their OWN life;
- the line is short;
- a picture anchors it.

Lines that hide the WRITER's private facts ("The AI kept moving Saturn") never qualify. "Boring is beautiful" works after a list of boring habits. "So you're
dead... my condolences" works because the picture shows exactly what is meant. If you have to
explain why it is clever, cut it. [CORPUS: harvest_simplicity_masters.md, harvest_reels_shorts.md]

## Voice: a person talking, not a deck and not a riddle

| Voice | Example | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Riddle | "One sky, two ways to count it." | Never: it hides the meaning |
| Deck | "It finds good hours to begin, and ones to avoid." | Avoid: clear, but nobody is talking |
| Person, direct | "Starting something new? Panditji tells you which hours are good for it." | Yes |
| Person, warm | "My family always asked a Panditji before starting anything. I built one you can ask from anywhere." | Yes |
| Person, witty | "It won't tell you when you'll die. Or which coin will 10x." | Yes, when the joke lands in one glance |

The marks of the person voice:
- **"I" and "you".** Sparsh made it, and you can use it.
- **A real situation.** "I can't code", "my first app", "I tried this on my own wedding
  photos".
- **A small, honest aside**, now and then.
- **Talk like a person.** Questions people actually ask, contractions, and the words Sparsh
  would use over chai.

Feature lists are still fine, but each feature is said TO someone: "You can ask it about
your own chart", not "It answers questions from your chart." The deck voice has a precise
signature: "It" or "The AI" as the subject, doing something habitually, with no person
anywhere ("It reads your day", "It finds good hours"). Third-person lines about a named person
read fine ("This legend built an app to help your grandparents").

This is still Tangent's calm register: no hype words, no shouting, no exclamation-mark
energy. Calm means a thoughtful person talking plainly, not a curated line on a gallery wall.
[RESEARCH-BACKED: Mayer's personalization principle, d about 0.79; CORPUS: every creator who
wins with a general audience; READER FEEDBACK: "not as if the owner is talking to his
audience"]

## Where the style goes instead

Tangent stays dark, quiet and classy. That comes from:
- **The look:** the photograph, generous space, one display face used for one line, restraint.
  Premium accounts stay simple by holding back (Visualize Value: one subject, mostly empty
  space, a caption that states the claim plainly).
- **The voice:** warm, first person, honest about limits, with a light human aside that lands
  instantly ("I can't code, so I asked Claude").
- **The proof:** real screenshots, real before/after, real numbers with their nouns.
- **Contrast you can read.** Body text is white or near-white on dark, or near-black on
  light. Never dim grey, and never coloured text for anything that must be read. Accent
  colour is for one highlighted word at most. Text-heavy slides may use a light background:
  display-polarity research generally favours dark text on light for reading (Piepenbrock et
  al. 2013), and the first-time reader asked for "more white, less black". Light against
  dark is a live test in `strategy/BETS.md`. Sparsh decides.

Not from compressed, riddle-like lines.

## The process (every artifact, pipeline or ad-hoc)

This order comes from a senior communications professional's feedback on our carousels:
message first, then the arc, then a check that the arc reads as a story, and only then the
content.

1. **Message Brief** (`templates/message-brief.md`): the post's one job, the one-line
   message, the audience, and a 3-5 beat arc. Say the beats out loud as the version you'd tell
   a friend. Never start from a slogan and decompress.
2. **Story check on the arc ALONE, before any copy.** Read only the beat sentences, top to
   bottom.
   - Do they read as one story, with someone, a problem, a turn and a result?
   - Does each beat follow from the last?
   - Could a stranger say what the post is about after hearing just these lines?

   If not, fix the arc, not the wording. In INTERACTIVE mode, show Sparsh the message and the
   beats and get his nod here. It is far cheaper to fix the story now than after the slides
   exist.
3. **Write the copy** in the copy format (`templates/carousel.md` for carousels and stills;
   `templates/script.md` beat blocks for reels). Each slide or beat carries its beat sentence,
   and usually nothing else (see the text budget in `craft/carousels/structure.md`).
4. **Lint:** `python tools/clarity_lint.py check <copy file>`. Fix every FAIL. Read the
   headline-only read: do the headlines alone tell the story? Read every line aloud: does it
   sound like Sparsh talking to one person, or like a slide in a deck?
5. **One light cold read:** `/cold-read`. Two fresh readers (persona A and persona B) who
   know nothing skim the post like real scrollers and say it back. It passes when they:
   - got the point;
   - felt it was one story;
   - would stop;
   - misread nothing on the cover or in a headline.
6. **Show Sparsh:** the one-line message, the beats, and both cold readers' restatements, next
   to the post. AI readers are more patient than people: they read every word and still
   restate correctly. A real first-time reader's verdict outranks theirs.

**Keep the checks light.** They exist to catch a post strangers don't get, like the deleted
carousel. They are not a polish loop. Readers always flag something, so take the fixes that
matter to a real viewer and move on. Grinding round after round on small wording snags
overfits to two AI readers and sands the voice flat. The final judges are real people: Sparsh,
a first-time reader, and the audience.

In the pipeline, `tools/pipeline.py` checks steps 1 and 5: BRIEFED needs the message, and G1
needs one `COLD-READ: PASS`.

## Worked example: the failed carousel, rewritten

Message: "I built an astrology app where a normal program does the maths, so the AI can't
make up where the planets are." Audience: A first, B through the caption and link. Arc 4
(what went wrong, and the fix). Tone: person, direct.

| Slide | Was (riddle voice) | Now (person voice; cold-read round 3) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The AI kept moving Saturn. So I took the maths away from it. | My free astrology app won't let the AI make up where planets are. |
| 2 | One sky, two ways to count it. | At first, my app's AI gave two answers to one question. |
| 3 | The phone does the maths. The model only explains it. | That's because AI chatbots don't work things out. They repeat what they've read. |
| 4 | Checked against the sky's reference books. | So now my app works out where each planet is. |
| 5 | 427 ways to ask the wrong thing. Tested. | Then I checked my app's answers against NASA's planet data. They match. |
| 6 | The full story, with every number, is on Tangent Labs. | Try Panditji free, or read how I built it. Link in bio. |

What the cold reads showed:
- **The original:** a fresh everyday reader would not stop, flagged 22 phrases and would have
  left by slide 4.
- **The first plain rewrite:** still read as "a presentation or lecture". The 24-degrees
  zodiac lesson did that, and so did a cover ("isn't allowed to guess") that left the object
  unclear. That was a real fix worth making.
- **After that:** both readers said the message back correctly, felt a person talking, and
  would save the post and send it on.

The later rounds spent on small snags were overfitting, and the checks were loosened because
of it. The full record is in `productions/2026-11-panditji-article-carousel-v2/`.
