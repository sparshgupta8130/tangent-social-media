# Vendor Evaluation: Hedra (and Alternatives) for the Param Vir Chakra Web-Series

**Date:** 2026-08-02 · **Requester:** Showrunner, for Sparsh's paid-tool decision · **Question:** is Hedra (or an alternative) worth subscribing to as a component of the PVC pipeline, given the recurring-character-consistency problem BET-5 flags and the real/deceased-persons sensitivity `ops/council/consults/2026-08-01-pvc-ethics.md` already cleared on the trust/legal side.

Methodology: WebSearch/WebFetch against official vendor terms/policy/pricing pages first, two parallel research passes covering HeyGen+D-ID and Runway+Kling, third-party reviews only to fill gaps (flagged). Confidence tags **[HIGH]** = official/primary page, directly fetched · **[MED]** = official page via secondary extraction, or named-methodology secondary source · **[LOW]/[UNVERIFIED]** = aggregator/blog, unreconciled, or convergent-but-unconfirmed. Every claim carries a URL + access date. This brief reads alongside `research/briefs/revamp-2026-08/products_teardown.md` §Hedra (cited as `[TEARDOWN]` below, do not re-derive what it already establishes) and is bound by `ops/council/consults/2026-08-01-pvc-ethics.md` (cited `[ETHICS]`) — that memo cleared the *trust/legal* question (dramatization is permissible with disclosure, family consultation, no voice-cloning); this brief answers a different, additive question: do the *vendors' own contracts* let Sparsh actually build it there. The two questions are genuinely separate: `[ETHICS]` already found Indian right-of-publicity law likely doesn't survive death (*Krishna Kishore Singh v. Sarla A. Saraogi*, Delhi HC 2021) — but legal permissibility and a vendor's own contractual Acceptable Use Policy are independent constraints, and this brief's central finding is that the vendor constraint binds even where the legal one doesn't.

---

## POSITION

**Do not subscribe to Hedra, HeyGen, or D-ID for this lane under their current self-serve terms.** All three contractually require consent from the depicted real person before generating their likeness, with **no living/deceased distinction that helps here** — Hedra's policy is the sharpest: it bans inputting *or generating* any media "containing individuals other than yourself," with a single exception for people **dead 100+ years**. The Param Vir Chakra award began in 1950; no recipient can satisfy that threshold before the year 2050. This is not a gray area to argue around — it is a bright-line contractual violation risk (account suspension/termination) that exists independent of whether the content itself is ethically fine per `[ETHICS]`. HeyGen's policy is functionally the same trap (consent required from "the individual represented," no exception at all for deceased subjects — literally impossible to satisfy). D-ID's language is thinner and puts the consent burden on the user rather than verifying it — but D-ID's own core technology has the single most on-point precedent found anywhere in this research (MyHeritage's Deep Nostalgia, built on D-ID, drew sustained backlash specifically over "how a deceased person might be considered to have consented" — exactly PVC's fact pattern).

**Runway and Kling are architecturally more interesting but not currently cleaner on policy**, and Kling has an unresolved, more severe risk: convergent (but unverified — Kling's own legal pages could not be fetched) secondary reporting describes an automated, frame-level guardrail blocking generation of "recognizable public figures" — potentially a hard technical wall, not a contract clause to negotiate.

**The strongest option is "no vendor."** Google's own Generative AI Prohibited Use Policy — the policy actually governing Flow/Veo — prohibits "impersonating an individual (living or dead) **without explicit disclosure, in order to deceive**" `[HIGH — policies.google.com/terms/generative-ai/use-policy, fetched 2026-08-02]`. That is a *disclosure-conditioned* standard, not a consent-from-the-specific-person standard — and disclosure-first is already Tangent's constitutional non-negotiable #2. This is the single most decision-relevant finding in this brief: Google's written policy is structurally compatible with respectful, disclosed historical dramatization in a way none of the avatar vendors' policies are. Google AI Ultra ($199.99/mo) is already in the stack — the marginal cost of this path is **$0**. The tradeoff is real but narrower than it looked before this research: Veo 3.1's Jan-2026 "Ingredients to Video" update already closes a meaningful share of the cross-scene consistency gap that originally motivated evaluating Hedra (see §2). What remains unverified is whether Google's *automated content filters* (tightened for "identifiable public figures" in the Nano Banana 2 update, Feb 2026) will actually let a recognizable likeness of a named recipient through in practice — that is a testable execution risk, not a policy-text risk, and it is the first thing a trial should check.

## §1 — Hedra current state

**Model**: Character-3 (Mar 2025) remains live; **Hedra Omnia** (Feb 2026) is the current flagship, full-body/facial animation from a single image with camera control; Character-4 is unreleased as of this research date (unofficial source only) `[MED — hedra.com/blog/ai-models-first-half-2026 + secondary; LOW on Character-4 status, sourced to a single X post]`.

**Capabilities**: 540p/720p/1080p, aspect ratios 1:1/16:9/9:16, 8 credits/sec baseline (higher for longer clips/resolution), ~19 min typical generation time `[HIGH — hedra.com/models/video/hedra/character-3, fetched 2026-08-02]`. Lip-sync is phoneme-aligned with micro-expressions/gaze tracking, reviewed as genuinely strong `[MED — convergent third-party reviews]`. **Character-3/Omnia is a single-character, close-camera talking-head tool by design** — multi-character dialogue requires generating each character separately and stitching in post; a "2 characters talking" feature request is still open on Hedra's own feedback board, and audio-based speaker-switching is a newer, narrower feature, not full scene composition `[MED — hedra.featurebase.app, hedra.com/blog, accessed 2026-08-02]`. This confirms `[TEARDOWN]`'s read: Hedra is a narrow generation point-tool, not a scene/pipeline product (no scheduling, no analytics — `[TEARDOWN]` §1).

**Pricing** `[HIGH — hedra.com/pricing, fetched 2026-08-02]`: Free (watermarked) → Basic $15/mo (1,500 credits ≈ 3.1 min/mo of Character-3 output at 8 cr/sec) → Creator $30/mo (5,400 credits ≈ 11.25 min/mo, "Popular") → Professional $75/mo (14,400 credits ≈ 30 min/mo) → Enterprise custom. All paid tiers grant commercial use. API launched Feb 2026, draws the same credit pool, incorporates the *same* Acceptable Use Policy by explicit reference `[HIGH — hedra.com/api-terms, fetched 2026-08-02]`. Live Avatar (real-time streaming, irrelevant to pre-recorded episodic content) is $0.05/min `[MED — secondary, not on the official pricing page]`.

**Content policy — the load-bearing finding, quoted verbatim** `[HIGH — hedra.com/acceptable-use, fetched 2026-08-02, two independent fetches consistent]`. Under "You agree not to use the Service... to input, generate, or create output that contains personal information related to any individual other than yourself, including (without limitation) any photos, audio, videos or any other media containing individuals other than yourself... **except that you may upload images of persons that died at least one hundred years ago** so long as you comply with applicable laws." Note the verbs: *input, generate, or create output* — this covers a purely text-prompted generation that merely results in a recognizable depiction of a named real person, not just photo-upload. A **separate** bullet bans deepfakes/impersonation "without consent" — but that consent carve-out does not attach to the personal-information bullet above; the 100-year threshold is the *only* stated exception, with no consent-based alternative path at all. No India-specific or military-content-specific language exists anywhere in Hedra's terms `[HIGH — same fetch]`.

## §2 — The actual need: does Hedra solve a gap Flow can't?

`knowledge/production/workload-router.md` and `research/briefs/revamp-2026-08/subscription_inventory.md` (both dated 2026-08-02, already in the knowledge bank) list "long-form multi-scene character/world consistency at scale" as Flow's honest gap for web-series production. That assessment predates a real update worth re-checking, which this task asked for:

**Veo 3.1 "Ingredients to Video" (shipped Jan 13, 2026, official)** `[HIGH — blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/ai/veo-3-1-ingredients-to-video/, fetched 2026-08-02]`: up to 3 reference images (character/object/scene) combined per generation; Google's own language: *"Identity consistency is better than ever... Keep your characters looking the same even as the setting changes, making it easier to tell a full narrative by having the same character appear across multiple scenes."* This is a genuine, dated improvement over the gap as previously logged — the memory-bank note should be treated as partially stale. Third-party claims of a specific "60-70% improvement" figure exist but are unlisted-tier and un-methodologied `[LOW — not cited as fact]`; direction is credible, magnitude is not.

**What this leaves as the real, narrower gap**: Veo/Flow is built for cinematic scene generation (camera movement, wide shots, native audio) with *reasonable-but-imperfect* per-shot consistency across separate generations — not for the specific close-camera, phoneme-tight lip-sync of a single character reciting or speaking that Hedra's Character-3 is purpose-built for. If the show's format leans on tight dialogue/narration close-ups of a recurring figure, Hedra's narrow specialty is real. If the format is closer to dramatized wide/medium-shot scenes (which the ethics memo's "actor-performed dialogue, reconstructing the documented act" framing suggests — `[ETHICS]` Q1b), Flow is already the better-matched tool architecturally, and Hedra's single-character-talking-head design is a poor fit for multi-character battlefield/regimental scenes regardless of the policy question. **Net: the gap Hedra would close is real but narrow (tight-lipsync hero moments), not the load-bearing production risk it might have looked like before this research — and it's the exact narrow use (recognizable close-up of the actual recipient) the AUP most directly blocks.**

**Runway detail worth separating from the table**: Act-Two is not a "type dialogue, animate a photo" tool like Hedra/HeyGen — it retargets a *filmed human performance* (an actual actor on camera) onto a character reference image, capturing full head/face/body motion. This is architecturally the closest of any vendor evaluated to genuine "actor-performed dramatization," which is exactly the model `[ETHICS]` Q1b requires. It still needs a character-reference image of the recipient to drive the retarget, so it still meets Runway's "without their permission" line — the policy silence on deceased/historical figures cuts both ways (no explicit ban, no explicit permission). Runway also trains on all input/output by default on every tier except Enterprise, with no opt-out `[MED — secondary, not independently verified against runway.com/terms which 404'd]` — a real consideration given the sensitivity of uploading war-hero reference material to a third party's training pipeline.

**Pricing conflicts flagged, not resolved**: D-ID's Advanced tier is reported as $196/seat/mo by one source and $299/mo by another (100 vs. 65 min) — unreconciled, D-ID's own pricing pages are JS-rendered and didn't yield tables on fetch. Kling's top consumer tier is reported as both $130 and $180/mo across sources. Verify both live before any commitment.

**Incidents/litigation, for context on exposure (none PVC-analogous, included because they were found in the same research pass)**: HeyGen's own CEO was impersonated in a crypto-scam deepfake ad `[MED — boingboing.net, Oct 2024]`; the AI Incident Database logs HeyGen (with ElevenLabs) used by scammers to deepfake real influencers without consent `[MED — incidentdatabase.ai/cite/683]`. Runway is a co-defendant in *Andersen v. Stability AI* — a training-data copyright class action, not a likeness/deepfake claim `[MED — copyrightalliance.org, Feb 2026 filing status]`. No lawsuit specifically naming D-ID or Kling for real-person-likeness misuse was found in this pass — absence of evidence given limited search depth, not a clean-record finding. None of this changes the vendor-policy analysis above; it's supporting texture on how seriously each vendor treats the underlying risk category.

## §4 — Recommendation

**Ranked, for the PVC lane specifically:**

1. **No vendor — Flow-only, with a character-bible discipline.** $0 incremental (Google Ultra already subscribed, `research/briefs/revamp-2026-08/subscription_inventory.md`). Workaround pattern: build a small set of Nano Banana Pro-generated reference stills per recipient (up to 5-person consistency already supported) as the "Ingredients" feed into Veo 3.1 across scenes; bias the shot list toward medium/wide dramatized coverage and cutaway/VO-driven narration rather than extended solo lip-synced close-ups, which is both where Flow is weakest *and* where a recognizable-real-person concern is sharpest. This is the only option whose governing policy (Google's disclosure-conditioned impersonation rule) is actually structured to fit this use case.
2. **Hedra/HeyGen/D-ID — do not subscribe under current terms.** If the format genuinely needs tight lip-synced hero moments badly enough to reconsider, the only defensible path is a direct written conversation with Hedra or HeyGen's enterprise/legal team asking for an explicit exception for disclosed historical dramatization — not assumed, not inferred from Enterprise marketing copy (neither vendor's enterprise page documents such a carve-out).
3. **Runway** — worth a hands-on trial *only* if Act-Two's real-actor-performance model is specifically valuable to the format; still carries the same "permission" line and a default no-opt-out training posture.
4. **Kling** — not recommended until the guardrail scope question is resolved by direct testing; the unverifiable ToS (every fetch blocked) is itself a due-diligence red flag independent of the content-policy question.

**Cost at ~2-4 episodes/month** (illustrative only — contingent on the policy blocker being cleared, which is not assumed):

**Policy-risk assessment for PVC specifically**: Hedra and HeyGen carry **contractual, bright-line risk** (a policy violation is essentially certain on the intended use, not a probabilistic read) — account suspension/termination exposure exists regardless of how respectfully the content is made, because the policy text doesn't engage with intent or disclosure at all, only with the depicted person's ability to consent. D-ID carries **reputational-precedent risk** (Deep Nostalgia backlash is the closest real-world analog found for "AI-animating a dead person's photo," and it went badly on the exact consent question). Runway carries **ambiguous-but-untested risk** (genuine policy silence, not a stated block). Kling carries **unverifiable, possibly-structural risk** (an automated filter, if the secondary reporting is accurate, isn't something a written request can negotiate around). Flow carries the **lowest textual risk** but an **unverified execution risk** (Nano Banana 2's Feb-2026 tightening on "identifiable public figures" — the automated filter, not the policy — may reject or degrade a recognizable likeness in practice; this needs a hands-on check, not an assumption in either direction).

**What a 1-week trial should test, in order**:
1. Generate a test likeness of a **non-PVC, already-public historical/deceased Indian figure** (not a specific recipient, to avoid live policy exposure while testing) through Flow/Nano Banana Pro + Veo Ingredients-to-Video, and see whether the automated filter blocks, degrades, or passes it — this resolves the one real unknown in the recommended path.
2. In parallel, email Hedra and HeyGen support/enterprise contacts directly, describe the disclosed-dramatization use case in writing, and ask whether any documented exception process exists — get it in writing before spending anything, don't infer from marketing copy.
3. If Runway is still of interest, test Act-Two's actual output quality end-to-end (a filmed test performance retargeted onto a stand-in character reference) to judge whether the architectural fit is worth the unresolved policy exposure.
4. Time the real generation-to-usable-take ratio (retries, failed generations) against the credit math above — every cost estimate in this brief assumes first-take success, which is optimistic.

## What would change my mind

- **A written exception from Hedra or HeyGen** covering disclosed, non-deceiving historical dramatization would flip the recommendation for the specific narrow "tight lip-sync hero moment" gap identified in §2 — nothing else changes that gap's existence.
- **A confirmed, direct answer on Kling's guardrail scope** (content-based vs. politically-scoped) — if it turns out to be narrowly China-political and not a general "recognizable real person" block, Kling's cost profile ($7-10/mo) would make it worth a real trial; right now the unresolved unverifiable-ToS problem alone is disqualifying.
- **A failed test in step 1 above** (Nano Banana Pro/Veo actually blocking or badly degrading a historical figure's likeness) would undercut the "no vendor" recommendation's practical viability even though its policy text is the most favorable — the whole recommendation rests on execution matching the written policy, which is untested as of this brief.

## Questions for Sparsh — these would change the recommendation

1. **What does a PVC episode actually look like visually?** If it's mostly narrator/VO over dramatized wide shots and archival-style material, Flow-only is a clean fit. If it leans on sustained close-up "the recipient speaks/reflects" moments, the case for a dedicated lip-sync tool (and thus for pushing harder on a Hedra/HeyGen exception) is stronger — this single fact changes the ranking more than anything else in this brief.
2. **How many recurring named figures per episode, and across how many episodes** — one recipient per episode (lower consistency burden) or an ensemble/anthology structure (harder, and where Veo's 3-reference-image ceiling could bind)?
3. **Is there already a planned actor/voice-performance layer** (per `[ETHICS]` Q1b's "actor-performed dialogue, never a voice clone")? If a real actor is already being filmed for performance capture, Runway's Act-Two becomes far more relevant than a straight avatar tool — worth knowing before dismissing Runway on cost alone.
4. **What's the tolerance for a slower, more manual production path** if Flow-only requires more shot-craft discipline (wider coverage, VO-forward) than a vendor's single-photo-to-talking-video shortcut would? This trades vendor cost for Sparsh's/the team's time.
5. **Has family/regimental consultation (`[ETHICS]` Q2) already started for episode 1's specific recipient?** If yes, that consultation could itself become the basis for a documented consent record to present to a vendor's enterprise/legal team — turning an abstract "ask for an exception" into a concrete, evidenced request.
