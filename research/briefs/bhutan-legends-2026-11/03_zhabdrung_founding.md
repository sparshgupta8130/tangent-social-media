## 1. Summary
1. He was NOT a prince or heir of Tibet. He was the Ralung hierarch of the Drukpa Kagyu school, from the family (rGya) of the school's hierarchs, and a claimant to be the rebirth of Pema Karpo. [HISTORY] (ARD p.12)
2. No uncle and no assassination plot appear in any source read. The dispute was with the Tsang ruler's court and a rival claimant, Pagsam Wangpo; the Tsang ruler demanded he hand over the sacred Kharsapani image. UNKNOWN beyond that; treat claim b as unsupported.
3. He left Tibet in 1616 (17th century), aged about 22. He had a tradition-level vision of the guardian Mahakala (Yeshe Gonpo) offering him the land, and Mahakala flew ahead as a RAVEN guiding him. No source read mentions a dragon dream.
4. "Druk" (thunder dragon) comes from the Drukpa school, named after a monastery whose founder heard thunder as a dragon's roar; it predates 1616 by four centuries. The dream is not the origin of the name.
5. The founding was a slow process (state declared c.1625-27, first dzong 1629, dual government 1640, repeated Tibetan and Tibeto-Mongol invasions repelled, eastern campaign from c.1643). He died in 1651; his death was kept secret for 54 years (to 1705, per Wikipedia/popular sources; the scholarly sources read say "more than fifty years").

## 3. The story as most accepted in Bhutan (about 650 words)

[HISTORY] Ngawang Namgyal was born in 1594 at Druk Jangchubling (Ralung area) in Tibet, into the line of the Drukpa Kagyu hierarchs descended from the school's founder, Tsangpa Gyare.[1] As a child he was recognised as the rebirth of the great master Pema Karpo (1527-1592) and was installed as hierarch of Ralung.[2] But Pema Karpo's line had passed outside the family for two lifetimes, and a rival claimant, Pagsam Wangpo, the illegitimate son of a powerful local lord, was backed by the rival camp and the Tsang ruler.[2][1]

[HISTORY] The quarrel centred on a relic, the Rangjung Kharsapani, an image of Avalokiteshvara said to have emerged from the cremated remains of Tsangpa Gyare and used to confirm each rebirth. The Tsang ruler wanted it handed over. The young hierarch refused, fearing it would be used to reject his claim.[2][3] In 1616 he left Tibet for Bhutan, carrying the relic. In Bhutan the Drukpa school already had patrons and monasteries; the textbook says that expansion "played an important role in receiving and assisting" him.[3]

[TRADITION] Bhutanese accounts say the protector Yeshe Gonpo (Mahakala) had appeared to him in a vision and offered him the country, and that on the journey the deity became a raven and flew ahead to show the way toward Thimphu.[4]

[HISTORY] He did not set out to found a state. Ardussi concludes his first aim was "to win his court case and return to Tibet".[2] In 1618 (ARD) or 1617 (TEXTBOOK) the Tsang ruler sent the first army; Bhutanese militias and local patrons (the Drukpa families of Phajo Drugom) defeated it.[2][3] Around 1623 he began a three-year retreat in a cave north of Thimphu, weighing two paths: wander as a hermit like Milarepa, or found a religious state like Phagpa of Sakya. Prophetic guidance and dreams of his late father pointed to the second.[2] In 1625/26 he announced a new government; in 1627 he composed the "Sixteen I" (Nga Chudrugma), a declaration of his authority, which became his seal.[2][3]

[HISTORY] In 1627 two Portuguese Jesuits, Cacella and Cabral, stayed with him for about eight months. Cacella called him king and chief lama and noted his abstinence.[5] In 1629 he began his first dzong at Simtokha.[3] In 1634 a Tibetan force, joined by rival Bhutanese lamas, took Simtokha but was crushed when the dzong's ammunition store caught fire.[3] He then built Punakha Dzong (1637-40 depending on source), where in 1640 he set up the dual system: a Je Khenpo for the monastic body and a civil head (later the Druk Desi).[3][6] More invasions came, in 1639, 1644 and 1648, and all failed; at Punakha in 1648 he is said to have ordered soldiers to march out of one gate and back in through another so the besiegers saw an endless army.[3][7]

[HISTORY] From c.1643 his lieutenants brought the east under the Drukpa state over about eight years.[3] He retreated to Punakha Dzong and died in 1651.[3][8] [TRADITION/HISTORY] His death was kept private for 54 years, so that orders could continue in his name.[8][9]

Reading: the lead's guesses (1634, 1639, 1644, 1648) match the Bhutanese textbook and Kinga; Wikipedia differs by one year for the last two. Textbook counts five invasions: 1617, 1634, 1639, 1644, 1648 (Box 1.2). Ardussi says "several later ones over combined Tibetan and Mongol forces" without listing. Ardussi also doubts, per WP, that the 1634 fighting ended cleanly (WP Battle of Five Lamas, lead).

**Other disagreements**
- Founding year of the state: 1625/26 (ARD pp.11, 14), "around 1627" (KINGA p.5), 1626-27 vision and seal (TEXTBOOK), first dzong 1629. Say "late 1620s".
- Punakha Dzong: 1637-38 (WP) vs 1639-40 (KINGA p.1) vs "after completion" then dual system 1640 (TEXTBOOK). The lead's 1637 is the start date in WP. Safe: "begun late 1630s, completed about 1640".
- Simtokha: 1628 (WP infobox) vs 1629 (WP text, TEXTBOOK).
- Name of the Tsang ruler at the 1634 attack: Karma Tenchog Wangpo (TEXTBOOK), Karma Tenkyong (WP). Phuntsho Namgyal is the earlier ruler (c.1597-1621).
- Concealment: ARD "more than fifty years"; WP "54 years"; popular sources "until 1705". Some travel sites give odd shorter figures (rejected, unsourced).
- Which lama invited him: "Hobtsho Lam" (bhutanpilgrimage, tourism), "Lama Goen Woptsho" (a Buddhistdoor article, page 403 here, snippet only), "Gasa region lamas" (travel). The scholarly sources read name no inviter; the lead's "Gasa Obtsho family" is UNVERIFIED.
- The vision: Mahakala vs "Mahakali" (a tourism spelling). Dream (popular) vs vision (KINGA). Direction: "southward" (popular), "towards Thimphu" (KINGA).
- The scholarly framing of the biography: Ardussi stresses the main Bhutanese biography (by Tsang Khenchen, c.1675) is "a political document" (ARD p.15) written to justify the state, so battle and magic claims are partisan.
- Textbook 'prince'? No. Textbook describes him only as hierarch.

## 8. Sensitivities
- Most revered figure; refer to him as Zhabdrung Rinpoche or "the Zhabdrung". Not "warlord". Magic and rituals are central to Bhutanese accounts; present as faith ("is said", "Bhutanese accounts say").
- Concealment of death: phrase as "the people closest to him kept his passing from the public for 54 years so the young state would hold together and orders could go out in his name; they said he was in a long silent retreat." Sources give a stabilising motive ("to forestall a dynastic struggle", WP-NN lead). Do not say "cover-up" or "hoax".
- Zhabdrung Kuchoe: death anniversary on the 10th day of the 3rd month of the Bhutanese calendar, a government holiday (BBS https://www.bbs.bt/?p=147792; the BBS item also notes a week of prayers led by the Je Khenpo). Gregorian date moves (April or May; one listing 26 April 2026, inconsistent, not relied on).
- Avoid Bhutan-Tibet-China framing. The textbook itself treats the Tibetan invasions as part of a Drukpa/Tsang and Drukpa/Ganden Phodrang rivalry, and Kinga stresses the Tsang ruler and the rival lamas. Frame as a succession dispute within the Drukpa Kagyu world.
- Later Zhabdrung incarnations: flag only. WP: Bhutan's government recognised the line to the 7th (Jigme Dorji, d. 1931). Do not include.
- Driglam namzha: the textbook calls it the code of etiquette within "Driglam Choesum" without crediting him. A snippet of an academic paper says the Zhabdrung promulgated it in the 1637 "Supreme Law Code" and that claims for far older origins are weak (not opened; UNKNOWN as fact). Avoid attributing it to him in script without checking.
- The Domchoe has a pazap militia, re-enactment; keep visuals respectful.
- Do not name the 'Jewel in the River' ritual loosely: in Punakha Domchoe the relic is carried in procession in a cloak; treat as sacred.

## 9. Memorable details for a 5-minute script
1. The relic: Rangjung Kharsapani, an image of Avalokiteshvara said to have emerged from the 21st vertebra of Tsangpa Gyare's cremated remains (KINGA p.5). It is still kept at Punakha Dzong (ARD fn.x, citing Aris 1979).
2. The raven: Mahakala, as a raven, "kept flying ahead to give directions towards Thimphu" (KINGA p.8). [TRADITION]
3. The choice in the cave: three years of retreat north of Thimphu; a hermit like Milarepa or a state-founder like Phagpa; dreams of his dead father tipped it (ARD p.14).
4. The Sixteen I: "I am the executioner of false incarnations" and "I am the incarnation prophesied by the patriarchs" (TEXTBOOK Box 1.1); the one-line form is on Bhutan's national emblem (TEXTBOOK p.4).
5. The gunpowder at Simtokha in 1634: invaders took the dzong, then its ammunition caught fire (TEXTBOOK p.5).
6. The gate trick at Punakha, 1648: soldiers out one gate, back in another; "unusual smoke" and flight (KINGA p.7; TEXTBOOK p.7). Origin of the Punakha Domchoe, first held 1649 (KINGA p.7).
7. The Jesuits, 1627: Cacella noted he "never eats rice, meat or fish" (JBS 27, quoting Baillie 1999), while rice later fed his state (the same article).
8. The 54-year silence. Pair with Machen (WP Punakha: embalmed body in Machey Lakhang, casket not opened; lead).
