## A. Painting (lhazo, thangka, mural)

**Where painting sits.** Lhazo (painting) is one of the thirteen arts of zorig chusum; one portal says it covers "thangkas, walls paintings, and statues" and decoration on furniture and window frames [S1 `summary`]. The same search noted the thirteen arts were first formally categorised in the rule of Gyalse Tenzin Rabgye (1680-1694) [S1 `summary` of a search result, not an opened page]. The Institute for Zorig Chusum in Thimphu is the government school; the Thangka Development Committee (formed 2000) and the National Technical Training Authority's Quality Seal are named on the Global Inch page [S2].

**Institutional account (best source in this pack).** Dorjee Tshering (then director, Department of Culture) and Thinley Wangchuk (director, National Institute for Zorig Chusum), Smithsonian Folklife Festival 2008 program book [S24] (opened, raw text): zorig chusum are thirteen arts, "yigzo (calligraphy), lhazo (painting), jimzo (sculpture), lugzo (metal casting), troezo (gold- and silversmithing), shingzo (carpentry), tshemzo (tailoring and tapestry), tsharzo (bamboo and container work), shagzo (wood turning and lacquering), thagzo (weaving), dzazo (pottery), chakzo (blacksmithing), and dozo (masonry)." The Zhabdrung "invited artists from neighboring countries to refine the arts of clay sculpturing, painting, and calligraphy" and set a formal curriculum; a fourth desi "established a school of arts and crafts in the seventeenth century"; the Institute for Zorig Chusum in Thimphu was set up in 1971. On thangka: the artist "initially draws a complex grid of geometric lines on the cloth", and "Thangka geometry is taught according to very strict artistic principles from which there is little deviation." Purpose of Bhutanese art, in their words: "to express Buddhism and convey genuine life experiences"; "never considered merely decorative".

**Materials and process (one secondary source, opened) [S2].**
- Pigments named: "cochineal for red, lapis lazuli for blue, arsenic for yellow"; greens from arsenic mixed with lapis lazuli. Binder: animal-skin glue (size). Canvas soaked in lukewarm water with glue and lime and stretched on a wooden frame; the prepared surface is called "dashu". Walls get coats of lime and gum emulsion.
- Gold: gold-dust ink in manuscripts; thangkas may carry gold decoration; "artificial gold is a sign of a commercial piece".
- Procedure listed: prepare surface, sketch the central figure first then subsidiary figures, diluted base colours in several coats, shading and outlining, varnish, polish the back, mount on brocade, then consecrate.
- CONFLICT/CAUTION: an earlier "mineral palette" lead in the old draft (azurite, malachite, cinnabar, orpiment, shell white) is NOT confirmed by [S2], which names cochineal, lapis and arsenic (orpiment is an arsenic sulphide, which is INFERENCE). Treat "azurite/malachite/cinnabar" as UNKNOWN until a primary source is read.

**Iconometry (thig-tshad) and why proportions are fixed.** Sketching starts with a linear grid matched to the deity's dimensions; diagonals set the axes and canvas centre; six proportional classes ("Thig Chen, great lines") cover the Buddha, peaceful bodhisattvas, goddesses, tall and short wrathful figures, and humans [S2]. The National Museum at Paro is said to explain the iconometric rules [S2]. That the grid is taught with "little deviation" is sourced [S24]; why (the Buddha's body as a prescribed measure; doctrinal authority) is UNKNOWN in opened sources. A search summary says wrathful deities break "rules of iconometry" with exaggerated features [S1 `summary`]; this conflicts in emphasis with [S2], which gives wrathful figures their own measured classes. Report as a disagreement: use measured proportions for every figure and treat "exaggeration" as measured, not free.

**Bhutanese vs central Tibetan style.** Search summaries (not opened pages): Himalayan Art Resources' glossary page (opened) says only: "the unique colour, composition and iconography of paintings in Bhutan" (no detail, no image licence statement) [S3a]; a SOAS paper says motifs shared by Drukpa Kagyu are "found in, but not distinctive of" Bhutanese painting; one source says thangka painting spread in the Zhabdrung's time when he invited Tibetan artists; another says Bhutanese painters probably received Menri/Khyenri (Menthang) and Karma Gadri styles; Jeff Watt (HAR) says those older traditions are not currently existing styles [S3 `summary`]. A Buddhistdoor interview was returned as saying Bhutanese work keeps Tang-era elements; the page returned 403 so this is unverified. The line between Bhutanese and Tibetan style, as an illustrator needs it (palette, line weight, landscape): **UNKNOWN**. Do not claim a Bhutanese-specific colour rule.

**Cloud / flame / water / rock conventions, landscape, gold line.** UNKNOWN from opened sources. Study the images in Section H (Four Friends, Wheel of Life, Vaisravana murals; the c.1500 Central Tibetan Mahakala; the mid-17th-century Shakyamuni) and let a Bhutanese painter confirm. Any rule written here would be memory, so none is given.

**Thongdrel.** The images in H show the Guru Rinpoche thongdrel at Jakar tshechu 2013 being unfurled and hung (Commons captions: "unfurling the Guru Rinpoche thongdrel", with "the Guru, his two wives and eight manifestations") [H1, H2]. ## B. Architecture

**Dzong (Wikipedia `[LEAD]`, opened via fetch summary, not an institutional source) [S4].** "High, inward-sloping walls of brick and stone" with few or no windows low down; "whitewashed inside and out"; a "broad red ochre band at the top on the outside"; "unique style flared roofs atop interior temples" built "without the use of nails"; shingles largely replaced by galvanised iron; courtyards; "massive entry doors made of wood and iron"; built without plans under a lama's direction; the style "reached its zenith in the 17th century" under the 1st Zhabdrung. The page does not use the terms utse, khemar, rabsel or bazam. These terms are therefore UNKNOWN as sourced definitions, although "utse" and the bridge appear on the Punakha page below.

**Punakha Dzong `[LEAD]` [S5].** Begun 1637, finished 1638, built by Ngawang Namgyal; sits "between the Pho Chhu (Male) and Mo Chhu (Female) rivers" at about 1,200 m; six-storey central tower called the utse; three courtyards; a covered wooden cantilever bridge over the Mo Chhu built in the 17th century, washed away by flood in 1957, rebuilt in traditional style in 2008 with a 55 m span. The Punakha Dromche reenacts the 1639 repulse of the Tibetan invasion. The "built 1637" detail matches the brief; "1637-38" is the page's wording. Floods damaged the dzong in 1957, 1960 and 1994.
Images: [H confluence, bazam entrance, dzong exterior].

**Paro Taktsang `[LEAD]`, Wikipedia (summary) [S27].** Monastery formally built 1692 "by the 4th Druk Desi Tenzin Rabgey" around the Taktsang Senge Samdup cave; the site was a meditation cave earlier; "hangs on a precarious cliff at 3,120 metres ... about 900 metres above the Paro valley"; four main temples and residences cut into granite, joined by stone steps and some wooden bridges; fire on 19 April 1998 in the main building, restored by 2005. Legend: Padmasambhava "flew to this location from Singye Dzong on the back of a tigress" (a second version has Yeshe Tsogyal as the tigress). INFERENCE: the 1692 date means a 17th-century story set before that should show the cave and cliff, not the later complex.
**Simtokha Dzong `[LEAD]`, Wikipedia (summary) [S28].** Founded 1628 or 1629 (page gives both) by Zhabdrung Ngawang Namgyal; "the oldest dzong which has survived in its original form"; three floors; single entry gate; about 300 slate carvings; a main lhakhang with a large Sakyamuni flanked by eight bodhisattvas; name said to come from "simmo" (demoness) and "do" (stone).
**Ralung `[LEAD]`, Wikipedia (summary) [S29].** In Tsang, Gyantse County, south of the Karo Pass, just north of Bhutan's Gasa district; founded 1180 by Tsangpa Gyare; "the traditional seat of the Drukpa Lineage"; Zhabdrung Ngawang Namgyal was its 18th abbot and fled in 1616 after the governor of Tsang challenged his recognition (uncited on the page). Destruction and rebuilding: not on the page (the Commons caption for the kumbum says destroyed in the Cultural Revolution, unverified).
**Temples across Bhutan (Karma Ura, Centre for Bhutan Studies) [S26].** "There are about 2,000 temples in Bhutan"; statues "contain scriptural teachings of the Buddha and body relics of eminent Buddhist masters".
**Lhakhang, chorten types, prayer flags, prayer wheels.** Date/founding/layout text: UNKNOWN (no institutional page opened). Photos exist in H: Simtokha doors, garuda and prayer wheel; Taktsang from four angles; Ralung kumbum ruin (destroyed in the Cultural Revolution per the Commons caption, which is an unverified caption) and murals (1993 photos); Kurjey and Jambay lhakhang; Druk Wangyal chortens at Dochula (modern, 21st century; do not use in a 17th-century scene) [H]. Colours and meanings of lungta vs manidhar flags: UNKNOWN.

## C. Dress

- **Gho/kira history (Daily Bhutan, Chow Ping, 27 May 2026) [S30]:** "introduced in the 17th century by Zhabdrung Ngawang Namgyel"; the gho is a knee-length robe "similar to the Tibetan chuba", typically plaid or striped; "solid reds and yellows are avoided" because they are linked to monks and royals. Kira change: 1930s "a single rectangular cloth" with calf-length hem; ankle-length from the 1970s; bold florals in the 1970s. The 17th-century cut is not described: UNKNOWN.
- **Kabney/rachu by rank (Daily Bhutan, Ashley Chen, 18 Dec 2020) [S31]:** "The rank of the bearer will determine the colour of kabney or rachu". Men's kabney: white for commoners; white with fringes and a red band (1, 2 or 3 stripes) for district officials; fringed with two red borders for village heads (gups; the page is internally inconsistent); dark blue for National Assembly/Council members; dark green for judges; orange without fringes for ministers and deputy ministers; red or red-gold for dasho; saffron for the King and the Je Khenpo. Women's rachu: colourful with intricate design for commoners. Disagreement: [S30] says the King's kabney is yellow, [S31] says saffron. Whether any of these ranks existed in the 17th century: UNKNOWN; do not assume the modern scale.
- **Gho** (men's knee-length robe tied with a belt, kera, over a tego jacket; worn with a kabney scarf on festive occasions); "Introduced in the 17th century by Ngawang Namgyal, 1st Zhabdrung Rinpoche"; modern legal form dates from 1989, but "the driglam namzha dress code is much older" `[LEAD]`, Wikipedia (summary) [S6]. - **Pazap dress at Punakha Drubchen:** a search summary (travel sites, not opened) says pazaps are men from the eight great clans of western Bhutan in warrior dress with swords, shields and armour, red and black attire, re-enacting the 1639 victory; sources differ on the date (1639 vs 1646). ## D. Symbols

- **Druk (thunder dragon) and the flag.** The flag is split diagonally, yellow upper, orange lower, white dragon on the line holding a jewel in each claw (Wikipedia, summary) [S8]. Meanings given: yellow = civil tradition and temporal authority of the Druk Gyalpo; orange = Buddhist spiritual tradition (Drukpa Kagyu and Nyingma); white dragon = purity; jewels = wealth and security; snarling mouth = deities' defence of the country. Another source (Daily Bhutan, summary) says "The yellow colour represents the yellow Kabney, the power of the King as the secular government" [S9]. Design credited to Mayum Choying Wangmo Dorji (1947), white dragon from 1956, orange lower half 1968/69, unchanged since 1969 [S8]; this originates in the Penjore and Kinga Centre for Bhutan Studies paper cited by Wikipedia, which I did not open.
- **Raven and Raven Crown.** Crown of the kings, "surmounted by the head of a raven"; "The bird represents a form of Mahakala, Bhutan's guardian deity" [S7]. The name "Gonpo Jarodongchen" is not on that page.
- **Eight auspicious signs (Daily Bhutan, Tshering Denkar, 13 Jan 2020) [S32]:** parasol "protects from suffering, destructive emotions, illness, harm and obstacles"; golden fishes "fearlessness, freedom and liberation"; treasure vase "inexhaustible source of long life, wealth, and prosperity"; lotus "purity of mind and heart"; conch "far-reaching melodious sound of the spiritual teachings"; endless knot "interdependence"; victory banner "victory over all disagreement, disharmony or obstacles"; wheel "the teaching of Buddha". Search summaries say traditions interpret them differently.
- **National symbols (a commercial tour operator, so low authority) [S33]:** the dragon gives the country its name; the raven "represents Gonpo Jarodongchen", chief guardian deity, and ornaments the royal crown; takin (national animal, around 4,000 m); blue poppy (national flower, above the tree line, about 1 m tall); cypress (national tree, near religious buildings, 1,800-3,500 m); emblem: a circle with double-diamond thunderbolt over a lotus, dragons and jewels at the sides (lotus = purity, thunderbolt = harmony of secular and religious power). Disagreement: search summaries list three species names for the blue poppy (Meconopsis grandis, M. gakyidiana, M. horridula); treat as "a blue Meconopsis".
- **Four friends.** The Smithsonian Folklife catalogue entry (summary) describes the Four Harmonious Friends as "elephant, monkey, rabbit, and peacock" from "a Bhutanese tale about cooperation" [S34]; other sources say partridge or bird (disagreement; the Commons caption for the Punakha mural says "the bird"). Search summary: elephant at the base, bird on top.
- **Four guardian kings, wheel of life, vajra and bell, tak seng chung druk.** No opened text source for meanings, colours or directions. Karma Ura notes that Guru Rinpoche is "depicted holding a dorje (thunderbolt), which symbolizes the energy and strength required to defeat ignorance" [S26]. What exists in images: Four Friends mural at Punakha (caption: "the bird plants a seed, the rabbit waters it" and so on) [H]; Vaisravana mural at Punakha [H]; Wheel of Life mural at Trongsa [H]; garuda and snow lion carvings at Simtokha [H]; Dorje Drolo on a tiger [H]. Meanings: **UNKNOWN**, to be filled from Bhutanese sources.

## E. Music and sound

Primary sources opened: the Folkways liner notes by Kheng Sonam Dorji, founder of the Music of Bhutan Research Centre (MBRC) (2013) [S10] and by J. S. Szuszkiewicz (1978) [S11].

**Genres [S10].** Zhabdro dangrem (also called zhungdra in a generic sense): "Bhutan's oldest folk music genre ... highly ornamented long phrases sung at slow tempo." Dorji says the term zhungdra properly belongs to a few Punakha dangrem performed "since the 17th century at religious festivals in Punakha". Structure: opening repeated phrase "lugo", then improvised section "zhey-yar" played an octave higher. Accompanied by drangyen, donglim, zurlim, kongtha (bamboo harp), chiwang, or unaccompanied, or with dance at tsechu. Zhabdro gorgom: "sung much faster", includes boedra ("Tibetan melody"; adapted from Tibetan boezhey) and drukdra (Dzongkha-language gorgom), often danced in circle or line. Lu-saar / rigsar: late 20th century, Western instruments. Tsangmo and lozey: only a Wikipedia lead says they are sung couplets, "ornaments of speech" `[LEAD]` [S12].

**Cham (institutional).** Lopen Lugtaen Gyatso (director, Institute of Language and Cultural Studies), Smithsonian Folklife program book 2008 [S25]: the major festivals are "domchoes and tsechus"; the highlight "is the religious dances performed by monks and laymen in fabulous costumes and masks", with atsara clowns between dances; of the drametse ngacham (UNESCO-recognised 2005): "The dancers wear spectacular, colorful costumes and masks representing real animals and mythical beings. All the masks symbolize the wrathful and peaceful deities of the pure lands" and "The dancers become manifestations of these deities"; sixteen perform and ten play music, twenty-one parts, over two and a half hours. INFERENCE: masks and cham are sacred performance, in line with BICMA 2.4.2 on "religious masks, sacred costumes".
**Cham (Folkways).** "The sacred music of Bhutan is performed together with cham dances ... attending a performance of sacred music and dance is considered an act of spiritual devotion" [S10]. Szuszkiewicz describes the Tsholing dance as masked deities "alarmed and saddened by the deeds of the sinners" [S11]. Monastic chant: UNKNOWN beyond "religious music is usually chanted" `[LEAD]` [S12].

**Ritual vs casual (INFERENCE from [S10], [S11], [S25] and F1).** Sources say sacred music is tied to cham, tsechu and temple ritual, and BICMA's content rules restrict the use of religious masks, sacred costumes and iconography in any medium without approval (F). No opened source speaks to ritual sound as background score. Cautious reading: do not sample, imitate in detail or loop dungchen, gyaling, kangdung, nga/rolmo and monastic chant as underscore; use secular instruments (lute, fiddle, flute) in an original score. Ask a Bhutanese musician to confirm.

## E2. Guidance for an original score

- Scale: "Bhutanese music is based on the pentatonic scale" (Szuszkiewicz, 1978) [S11]; Dorji notes the ascending and descending pentatonics of raga bhupali derived "from the music of Bhotadesa" per an Indian scholar [S10]. Which pentatonic (the intervals): UNKNOWN. Drangyen tuning G-C-F is given in [S10] (tuning, not mode).
- Tempo: dangrem is "sung at slow tempo" with "highly ornamented long phrases"; gorgom is "much faster" [S10]. No BPM: UNKNOWN.
- Practical, INFERENCE: for slow-scroll shots, write original slow ornamented melody on plucked/bowed/flute timbres in a pentatonic; leave ritual instruments and chant out; credit "original score, inspired by Bhutanese folk idiom".

## F. Rules and law

Not legal advice. Flag for the owner: written confirmation from BICMA.

**F1. BICMA Rules and Regulations on Content, 3rd edition, in force 1 July 2019 (opened PDF) [S14].**
- Scope, clause 1.3: "shall apply to all content made available by the ICT and media industry within the Kingdom of Bhutan".
- Clause 2.4.2 (Religion and Culture): "The use of religious masks, sacred costumes and iconography including the national emblems in any medium shall not be permitted except when used in proper context and with prior approval from the appropriate government authority."
- Clause 2.1(a): content is measured "in the context of the country's social, religious, political and cultural requirements". Clause 2.2(b): not "incite religious, ethnic, regional or communal conflict".
- "Content" is defined broadly (any picture, still or moving, in any form), and the Authority interprets the rules (1.6).
- Whether 2.4.2 reaches a foreign animation published abroad on YouTube/Instagram: the rules say they apply to content "within the Kingdom of Bhutan" and to the "ICT and media industry"; the text does not say what happens with a foreign creator. **UNKNOWN**; ask BICMA.

**F2. Bhutan Filming Regulation (2007, amended 19 March 2025) (opened PDF) [S15].**
- Scope, clause 3: applies to "any filming activity in Bhutan".
- Clause 6(i): "Any activities related to films, documentaries and advertisements shall be allowed in Bhutan only after obtaining a permit from the Authority." International permit fee Nu 150,000 plus Nu 100,000 deposit (Annexure I).
- Clause 8.3: filming at "Dzongs, monasteries, national parks, mountain peaks and other protected areas" needs prior written permission.
- Clause 7.4: the Authority previews foreign documentaries/films made and may require changes. Clause 8.1(xiii): screening "shall not be done or allowed without first being reviewed by the relevant authorities".
- Reading: all of this speaks of filming in Bhutan. It does not mention animation or AI-generated moving image, and says nothing about works made abroad without footage. **Applicability to our series: UNKNOWN.**

**F3. Rules Governing Examination and Certification of Films (as amended 19 March 2025) (opened PDF) [S16].** Scope 1.3: "all Bhutanese filmmakers". 4.1: commercial feature films are examined; "documentary films shot in Bhutan" are reviewed. Aim: content of films "intended for public exhibition in Bhutan". Not on its face aimed at a foreign online animation; UNKNOWN in practice. Earlier precedent from the previous draft (Hema Hema denied certification for use of ritual masks) came from search summaries and is unverified here.

**F4. National flag.** National Flag Rules of Bhutan, 1972 (Resolution 28, 36th session, 8 June 1972) (WIPO Lex page via fetch summary) [S17]. "The signs of the national flag cannot be used as business logo" (s 8(e)) and "designs similar to the national flag cannot be imprinted on any other objects" (s 8(f)); the flag may not be used as wrapper or cover; it must not be hoisted upside down; torn flags cannot be hoisted. No penalties on the page. **INFERENCE:** do not use the flag design as merch artwork or as a logo; avoid showing the flag in a 17th-century scene anyway (C).

**F5. National emblem / images of the King and royal family.** BICMA 2.4.2 names "the national emblems" among items needing approval (see F1). A dedicated law or rule on images of the King or royal family: **UNKNOWN** (not found). Do not depict living royals; ask BICMA.

**F6. Photography inside lhakhangs/dzongs.** Search summary only (travel guides: interiors off-limits, courtyards usually OK; no Department of Culture rule found) `[LEAD]`. Filming regulation 8.3 (above) covers filming at dzongs and monasteries. Department of Culture rule text: **UNKNOWN**. Implication: do not reconstruct identifiable interior murals or statues from photos taken in violation; use open-licensed study images only (and never reuse them).

**F7. Copyright/ICH points on sacred imagery.** UNKNOWN (no Bhutanese copyright or ICH page opened).

## G. Etiquette for sacred imagery

Opened sources are thin; several of the queries returned only retailer blogs.
- FPMT (a Tibetan Buddhist organisation), Dharma etiquette page [S35] (fetch summary): dharma texts "should not be sat upon, stepped over or on, or placed directly on the floor" and are kept "in a high, clean place separate from more mundane materials"; photos or images of holy beings should "not be burned" but "placed with respect in a stupa, tree, or other high, clean place". The page does not address statues, thangkas, feet, above-the-waist or merchandising. A Jangchup Lamrim guidance page (search summary, not opened) gives the same floor/step-over rule for texts. For images as such (cropping through face or body, sitting above, merchandising): **UNKNOWN** in opened sources. Retail blogs (butuzou.com) were ignored as commercial. The Rigpa Wiki page returned 403.
- Karma Ura [S26]: a silk scarf "is worn as a sign of respect while polishing a statue of Guru Rinpoche in Gangtey Monastery"; temple statues "contain scriptural teachings of the Buddha and body relics", which is why they are venerated. INFERENCE: handle painted images with the same care until a Bhutanese reviewer says otherwise.

- Wrathful deities as protectors, not demons: no opened source states this; UNKNOWN. (The Commons caption for Dorje Drolo uses "wrathful emanation of Padmasambhava" [H].)
- **AI and sacred art.** Published views found and opened:
  - Boudha Stupa Thanka Center (Aug 2026, a commercial seller) [S20]: AI thangkas show errors: "a wrong arm count for Chenrezig", a Bhumisparsha mudra "onto a bodhisattva figure where it doesn't belong", "blending attributes, colors, or postures". Cause: "AI image generators are trained to recognize visual patterns and aesthetics, not the strict, centuries-old iconometric rules." No lama or painter cited, and the author has a sales interest. It also says "What has always mattered is intentionality" for trained human innovators.
  - "Why We Shouldn't Use AI to Caricature the Buddha or Buddhist Monks Inappropriately", Knowing Buddha Org (undated) [S21]: concerns degrading edits (inappropriate clothing, swapped heads) that "constitute an extreme insult to the Buddha"; says nothing about iconography errors.

  - Lion's Roar, 26 May 2024 [S23]: Tergar Asia and Dongyu Gatsal Ling Nunnery warned of AI deepfake videos using teachers' likenesses. This concerns impersonation of living teachers, not sacred art. Relevance: do not give any real lama a voice or face.
  - Critical views of the opposite side (AI is acceptable if handled respectfully): none found in opened sources; a fair summary is that only critical or cautionary views were found, and the only Tibetan-specific piece is commercial. **No Bhutanese commentary on AI sacred art was found.** UNKNOWN for Bhutanese Buddhist authorities.

## Do / Don't (illustrator and composer)

1. DO paint inward-sloping whitewashed dzong walls with a red band under flared roofs (B).
2. DO set Punakha at the meeting of two rivers; the covered cantilever bridge is a 17th-century feature, the long suspension bridge is not (B).
3. DON'T put the Raven Crown, the national flag or the Dochula chortens in a 17th-century scene (C, D, B).
4. DON'T use the flag design as logo, merch print or wallpaper (F4).
5. DO give every sacred figure grid-based proportions and the correct attribute set; reject frames with wrong arm counts, mudras or attributes (A, G).
6. DON'T treat wrathful deities as monsters in captions or framing; no opened source covers this, so ask a Bhutanese reader (G).
7. DON'T invent colour rules for clouds, flames or rock; copy only what a Bhutanese painter confirms (A).
8. DON'T recreate identifiable interior murals or statues from photos; stay with openly licensed study images (F6).
9. DON'T give any living lama or royal a face or voice (F5, G).
10. DO score with secular instruments: drangyen, chiwang, flutes, in a slow ornamented pentatonic (E, E2).
11. DON'T use dungchen, gyaling, kangdung, nga, rolmo or monastic chant as casual underscore (E).
12. DO keep disclosure on screen and in captions; use YouTube's AI-use setting when anything looks realistic (F8).
13. DO keep a Bhutanese reviewer in the approval loop before publish (F, G).
14. DO ask BICMA in writing whether Section 2.4.2 applies to a foreign animation (F1).
