## 1. Summary
1. The best source found is Sonam Kinga, "Dumping the Jewel in the River", Journal of Bhutan Studies 49 (2023). It says the relic emerged from the twenty-first vertebra of Tsangpa Gyare, and that Zhabdrung carried it out of Tibet in 1616.
2. Tsang Desi demanded the relic and sent invasions. Fighting was real, with generals killed and "thousands" of Tibetans killed in one battle, so "won without killing anyone" is wrong as a blanket claim.
3. The river ruse is set at Punakha, on the Mo Chhu (not Pho Chhu). Oral tradition says Zhabdrung pretended to dump the relic and threw an orange as a decoy while the relic was hidden in his sleeve. Sources date the ruse differently (1639 per a Dratshang spokesperson in Kuensel; 1648-49 per Kinga and others).
4. The festival (Punakha Drubchen / Dromchoe) ends with a procession to the Mo Chhu where the Je Khenpo (or a senior monk) throws oranges and mandala sand. The relic itself is not thrown.
5. The access rule (King, Je Khenpo, caretaker) is documented for the Machen Lhakhang (Zhabdrung's embalmed body), not for the Kharsapani relic. No source found for who may enter the relic's room.

## 3. The story as told in Bhutan (tagged)

[HISTORY/TRADITION] In 1211 the Drukpa Kagyu founder Tsangpa Gyare Yeshe Dorje (1161-1211) was cremated. Tradition says twenty-one images of Avalokiteshvara, the Buddha of Compassion, appeared on his twenty-one vertebrae. The one on the twenty-first, the Rangjung Kharsapani ("self-arisen Avalokiteshvara"), is revered as the most sacred. (Kinga p.5.)

[TRADITION] Later Drukpa hierarchs kept it. It is relied on to authenticate the reincarnations of Drukpa leaders. (Kinga p.5.)

[HISTORY] Zhabdrung Ngawang Namgyal (b.1594) was recognised as the incarnation of Pema Karpo, but a rival claimant, Pagsam Wangpo, had the backing of the Tsang ruler. A clash at Tagdru left two of Lhodrak Pawo's attendants stabbed to death. Tsang Desi demanded compensation and then the relics. (Kinga p.4.)

[HISTORY] In 1616 Zhabdrung fled to Bhutan with the relic. Tsang Desi seized his estates and demanded the relic back. Zhabdrung's reply was strongly worded and the Desi sent a general. The invasion was repelled and the general killed; more followed (1634, 1639). (Kinga p.5.)

[HISTORY] After Tsang fell to the Gelugpa and Mongols (1642), Lhasa's new Desi dispatched forces in 1644. They entered Paro via Phari. Zhabdrung's militia disarmed them and sent them home, but kept twenty-one generals as hostages for two years. (Kinga p.6.)

[HISTORY/TRADITION] In 1648 a far greater force came in two columns, one via Gasa to Punakha and one via Phari to Paro. The Paro camp was stormed and "thousands of Tibetans killed". At Punakha, Bhutanese were inside the dzong and Zhabdrung told them: "Just keep open all the entrances to the dzong." Men went out of one gate shouting and came back through another, so the besiegers saw an endless army. The Tibetans besieged for three months, heard their general had fled, burned their camp and fled, and were engaged in the smoke. (Kinga p.6-7.)

[TRADITION] The oral account adds truce talks at Thangzang, the open ground in front of the dzong. Five Tibetan generals came without helmets or weapons. Bhutanese said the relic, the cause of the conflict, would be thrown in the river. A procession led by Zhabdrung "pretended to proceed and dump this precious jewel". Instead of the relic, "an orange as a decoy". The relic stayed in his sleeve. The Tibetans rebuked the Bhutanese for the foolishness and went home. (Kinga p.25-26.)

[TRADITION] Zhabdrung then held a thanksgiving ceremony in the first month of 1649, the first Domchoe, and it has been held annually since. (Kinga p.7.)

## 5. Variants and disagreements
- Vertebra: Kinga says 21st of 21; travel sites say "a vertebra". One search snippet (unread page) mentioned Manjushri images on vertebrae and skull: UNKNOWN, not opened.
- Which invasion: Kuensel 2006 (Dratshang spokesperson): 1639. Kinga and Wangchuk blog: 1648-49. Kinga ties the first Domchoe to 1649.
- Which river: all opened sources say Mo Chhu. Pho Chhu appears only in the owner's claim.
- What was thrown: an orange as decoy (Kinga oral account); "pretended" only (Windhorse Tours, travel); Wangchuk: "a full ceremony of the immersion... was staged".
- Written evidence: Kinga p.26 (via Aris p.618) says no written source mentions the dumping of the jewel, only disposal of mandala sand. Folk tradition vs monastic text.
- Dates of the Punakha dzong build: 1637-38 vs 1639-40.
- Who may enter: Kuensel 2008 says Kings, Je Khenpos, Machen Zimpon; Kuensel-based Zimpon page says Machen Zimpon and Soepon. Both are for the Machen, not the relic.
- Dharmaraja: Kuensel 2008 uses "Dharma King" for the King ("the ultimate empowerment of a Dharma King"). "Dharma Raja" is CONTESTED in usage: Wikipedia's Je Khenpo article (read, lead only, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Je_Khenpo) says the Je Khenpo was "formerly called the Dharma Raja by orientalists", while search summaries (pages not opened, e.g. a Mandala Desi text) say foreigners used Dharmaraja for the Zhabdrung incarnate. So both readings exist; neither verified from a scholarly page I could open. Recommend "Je Khenpo (chief abbot)" in the script.

## 7. Appearance and the festival
- Relic: "one face and two arms with lotus stalks in the left hand and the right hand in a position of protection" (Phub Dorji Wang blog, personal blog, lead only; http://phubdorjiwang.blogspot.com/2019/02/rangjung-kharsapani-self-created.html). Size: UNKNOWN. Published photograph: none found. Public display: Wikipedia (lead) claims it is displayed during the festival; not confirmed. Kinga p.24 says sacred national treasures are mounted over the mandala and covered "to be secluded from public view". Treat as not shown.
- Pazaps: eight communities (Kabji, Toepa, Bjimed, Toewang, Chang, Barp, Kawang, Mewang) plus Lingmukha; 136 in 2012. Outfit: headgear, swords, boots, outer wear called chari, provided by government since 1971. Metal knob on headgear said to hold cloth worn by Zhabdrung. Gups dressed as senior monks; abstain from sleeping with wives during festival. (Kinga p.11-12, 23)
- Rhythm: 15 days Goenwang Drubchen prayers inside the dzong, then three days of pazap displays; camps pitched two days earlier; war cries, firecrackers.
- Procession: monks in brocades, a monk carries the "jewel" wrapped in a cloak, another a parasol, a senior monk dressed as Zhabdrung in black hat and Mahakala robe (Kinga p.22-23). At the river: libation to nagas (glu chog), oranges thrown, multi-coloured mandala sand thrown; young men dive for oranges; oranges kept at home as blessed objects (Kinga p.23-25).
- 2005: Punakha Tshechu added; Kuensel 2006.

## 8. Sensitivities
- Do not depict the relic close-up; no authenticated image exists in what was read. Show only a cloaked bundle, as the festival itself does.
- The Machen holds Zhabdrung's body; do not show the interior or the body.
- Do not frame as Bhutan versus Tibet or China. Kinga frames the invaders as Tsang then Ganden Phodrang/Mongol forces; keep it as 17th-century rival Drukpa vs Gelug/Tsang politics.
- Zhabdrung expressed sorrow for the dead (Wangchuk blog, lead only); a respectful tone is supported.

## 9. Script details (5-minute video)
1. Twenty-one vertebrae, twenty-one images, one of them the most sacred (Kinga p.5).
2. "Just keep open all the entrances to the dzong" (Kinga p.7): the endless-army gate trick.
3. Five Tibetan generals came without helmets or weapons to talk truce (Kinga p.26).
4. An orange as decoy; relic in the flowing sleeve (Kinga p.26).
5. Young men strip and dive for the oranges; kept at home as blessings (Kinga p.25).
6. Monks do not sleep for fifteen days and nights, apart from an hour or two (Kinga p.9).
7. Pazap helmets hold a scrap of cloth Zhabdrung wore (Kinga p.23).
8. Folk memory vs record: no written text mentions the dumping of the jewel (Kinga p.26).
