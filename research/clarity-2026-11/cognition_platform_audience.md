## 1. Cognitive science of easy reading

### 1.1 Processing fluency raises liking, perceived truth and trust [RESEARCH-BACKED]
- Reber, Schwarz and Winkielman (2004), "Processing Fluency and Aesthetic Pleasure," Personality and Social Psychology Review 8(4):364-382. Claim: the more fluently a perceiver processes a stimulus, the more positive the response. Ease carries its own positive feeling ("hedonic marking"). Fluency is raised by figure-ground contrast, symmetry, repetition, prototypicality and priming. https://pages.ucsd.edu/~pwinkielman/reber-schwarz-winkielman-beauty-PSPR-2004.pdf (title and abstract confirmed via search; full text not fetched).
- Reber and Schwarz (1999), Consciousness and Cognition 8:338-342: perceptual fluency affects judgments of truth. Only the citation was confirmed (via the 2004 review). I could not verify the specific color-contrast manipulation.
- Alter and Oppenheimer (2009), "Uniting the Tribes of Fluency to Form a Metacognitive Nation," Pers. Soc. Psychol. Rev. 13(3):219-235, doi 10.1177/1088868309341564. Review: conceptual, perceptual and linguistic fluency all feed one "this is easy" signal that shifts judgments of truth, familiarity, confidence and liking. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1088868309341564 (abstract and metadata only).
- Evidence strength: strong and replicated in the lab, but effects are modest and context-dependent (fluency can be discounted if people are told the source of the ease). It is a nudge on liking and credibility, not a guarantee of attention.

### 1.2 Needless complexity makes you look less intelligent [RESEARCH-BACKED]
- Oppenheimer (2006), "Consequences of erudite vernacular utilized irrespective of necessity: Problems with using long words needlessly," Applied Cognitive Psychology 20(2):139-156. A majority of students admitted deliberately using complex vocabulary to seem smart. Across experiments, more complex text (long thesaurus swaps in grad-school admission essays) was rated as written by less intelligent authors, regardless of original quality. The effect was mediated by processing fluency, and hard-to-read fonts did the same. Won the 2006 Ig Nobel for Literature. Summary: https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/acp.1178 and https://www.dailyprincetonian.com/article/2005/11/simple-writing-best-study-finds . Oppenheimer's suggestion that liking and trust also suffer is speculation, not a tested result.
- This is the single most on-point study for Tangent's failure ("Checked against the sky's reference books", "One sky, two ways to count it"): decoding effort is read by the audience as a cue about the author and the idea.

### 1.3 Cognitive load theory (Sweller) [RESEARCH-BACKED]
- Working memory is limited. Intrinsic load comes from the material's element interactivity, extraneous load from how it is presented, germane load is effort spent building understanding. Loads add. Extraneous load (decoding, search, split attention) steals capacity from understanding. Overview: Paas, van Gog and Sweller (2010), Educational Psychology Review, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-010-9133-8 . I only saw secondary summaries of the framework in search. The categories are criticised as not always separable (Schnotz and Kurschner 2007).
- Working memory holds about 4 chunks (3-5), not Miller's 7 plus or minus 2. Cowan (2001) BBS and Cowan (2010) "The Magical Mystery Four," Curr. Dir. Psychol. Sci. 19(1):51-57. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2864034 . Contested: some argue 4 is too low or depends on what counts as a chunk.
- Learning is easiest for novices; Mayer's 2017 review notes several principles are stronger for low-knowledge learners (see 1.4). Tangent's general audience are low-knowledge by definition.

### 1.4 Mayer's multimedia principles [RESEARCH-BACKED]
Effect sizes below are medians from Mayer's 2017 review in the Journal of Computer Assisted Learning ("Using multimedia for e-learning"), as reported in a search summary; later book editions revise some values (coherence reported as 0.86 in the 2021 edition). Source index: https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_journals_1936208032 ; Mayer treats d above 0.40 as educationally meaningful. I could not fetch the full tables (UNH summary PDF failed on certificate). Laboratory, mostly short lessons, mostly education settings, not social feeds.

| Principle | What it says | d (2017) | Carousel / video translation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coherence | Drop interesting but irrelevant words, pictures, sounds | 0.70 | One idea per slide. Cut every decorative line, jargon aside and bonus fact. |
| Signaling | Cues that show what matters | 0.46 | Bold or colour the one key phrase. Number slides ("2 of 5"). |
| Redundancy | Do not duplicate the same words as on-screen text and narration | 0.87 | In video, do not caption word-for-word AND show a heavy text overlay saying the same thing. In carousels do not repeat the headline in the body. |
| Spatial contiguity | Put labels beside the thing they label | 0.79 | Label directly on the image, not in a legend or a separate caption. |
| Temporal contiguity | Present narration and visual together | reported d 1.30 (ambiguous in my snippet) | Say it while showing it. Show the screenshot on the beat of the sentence. |
| Segmenting | Break lessons into learner-paced parts | 0.70 | Carousel slides are natural segments; each swipe is learner-paced. |
| Pre-training | Teach names and key terms first | 0.46 | Define a needed term once, plainly, before using it, or avoid it. |
| Modality | Graphics plus narration beat graphics plus on-screen text | 0.72 | For Shorts, voice carries the explanation; keep on-screen text to a few words. |
| Personalization | Conversational "you" style beats formal | 0.79 | Write "you" and "I", not passive reporting. |
| Voice | Friendly human voice, not machine voice | 0.74 | Use his real voice, not a robotic voiceover. |
| Multimedia | Words plus pictures beat words alone | median 1.67 (2017); 1.35 (2021 summary) | Every slide carries a picture or screenshot of the thing. |

### 1.5 Curse of knowledge [RESEARCH-BACKED]
- Camerer, Loewenstein and Weber (1989), Journal of Political Economy, named the bias. Newton's (1990, Stanford) tapping study: tappers predicted listeners would name about 50% of songs; listeners got about 2.5% of 120. https://www.thedecisionlab.com/reference-guide/management/curse-of-knowledge (secondary summary; I did not reach the primary papers).
- Applies to Tangent directly: he has just finished building Panditji, so "deterministic code", "ephemeris" and "arcseconds" feel like plain words to him and not to anyone else. AI draft lines that "sound stylish" are also the output of a writer with no real audience in mind.

### 1.6 Concreteness effect and dual coding [RESEARCH-BACKED]
- Paivio's dual coding: concrete material is encoded both verbally and as an image; abstract material only verbally. Memory rises from abstract words to concrete words to pictures; roughly a 2:1 average advantage in Paivio's summary. Boundary: the concreteness advantage can shrink in free recall, and context availability theory (Schwanenflugel) competes as an explanation. Sources found via search: https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-018-0858-9 and https://clark.faculty.uwinnipeg.ca/Clark/Publications/1984-Clark-Repmem.pdf (not fetched in full).
- Practical consequence: "The AI kept moving Saturn" is abstract. "I asked the AI where Saturn was on my birthday. It gave a different answer every time." is concrete and picturable.

### 1.7 Curiosity needs a small, closeable gap [RESEARCH-BACKED, with caveats]
- Loewenstein (1994), "The Psychology of Curiosity," Psychological Bulletin: curiosity is the felt gap between what you know and what you want to know. It requires some prior knowledge (you cannot be curious about something you cannot perceive as missing) and is strongest for small, closeable gaps. https://stafforini.com/works/loewenstein-1994-psychology-curiosity-review/ (secondary summary only).
- Implication: a hook must reference something the viewer already has a picture of, then withhold one specific answer. "Decode this phrase" is not a curiosity gap; it is a work gap.

### Rules for Tangent writers (section 1)
1. A reader must get each slide in about one second with no decoding. If a phrase needs thought, it is wrong. (Oppenheimer 2006, fluency)
2. One idea per slide; delete anything not serving it (coherence, d about 0.70-0.86).
3. Use a word a 12-year-old already owns. Ban jargon unless it is the actual subject; if used, define it in plain words on first use (pre-training).
4. Always concrete and picturable: name the thing, the person, the number you can see. Replace "a way to count the sky" with what actually happened on screen.
5. Put the label on the picture it labels; never make people look between two places (spatial contiguity, split attention).
6. Write to "you" and "I" in conversational English (personalization, d about 0.79).
7. Self-test for the curse of knowledge: show each slide to someone with no context (or run a "can a 12-year-old restate this?" check). If they cannot restate it, rewrite.
8. Hook = a small gap about something the viewer can already picture, answered on the next slide.

## 2. Explanation craft

### 2.1 Made to Stick: SUCCESs and Commander's Intent [PRACTITIONER LORE, well regarded; the underlying memory-for-concrete-and-story claims are partly research-backed]
- Chip and Dan Heath, Made to Stick (2007): Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories. Method: (1) find the essence, (2) express it with the SUCCESs template. Summaries confirm the six traits (e.g. https://fourminutebooks.com/made-to-stick-summary/ ). The "Simple" chapter's core device, Commander's Intent (a one-line statement of the goal that survives changing circumstances), is from my background knowledge of the book; the search results did not confirm it (UNVERIFIED-PRIMARY).
- It is a synthesis book with illustrative cases, not experiments. Treat the six traits as heuristics.
- Tangent use: before every post, write one sentence: "After this, the viewer knows ___." That is the intent. If a slide does not serve it, cut it.

### 2.2 Axios Smart Brevity and the inverted pyramid [PRACTITIONER LORE]
- Smart Brevity (VandeHei, Allen, Schwartz, 2022): lead with the news in a short tease; "why it matters"; then "go deeper" for those who want more; show word count and reading time. https://www.fastcompany.com/90355858/axios-is-now-begging-you-to-finish-reading-its-newsletters (via search summary). No hard word limits; the authors call the rules guardrails. Critics call the book a marketing tool (Washingtonian review).
- Inverted pyramid: most important first, detail later, so a reader who stops early still has the point. This is exactly the two-layer design in section 6.4.

### 2.3 Plain language guidelines and grade targets [OFFICIAL / PRACTITIONER, with research-backed core]
- US federal plain language guide now lives at https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language (fetched; index page only, with sections "Writing for understanding", "Design for understanding", "Test for understanding"). I could not reach the specific writing sub-rules in this session.
- UK GOV.UK writing guidance: could not extract the plain English page (redirect chain returned navigation only). Do not quote GDS specifics from this file; confirm at https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/ . UNVERIFIED-PRIMARY.
- Experts prefer plain language too: NN/g (8 Oct 2017), "Plain Language Is for Everyone, Even Experts": usability study with scientists, technologists and medical specialists; highly educated readers "crave succinct information that is easy to scan"; jargon-laden pages were rejected. No sample size given. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/plain-language-experts/ [INDUSTRY/usability research, qualitative]
- Grade targets: Hemingway App defaults to grade 9 (average adult), states the historical US adult average is grade 8, and says low grade does not mean childish writing. A common plain-language target for public web copy is grade 6-8. https://hemingwayapp.com/help/docs/readability and https://hemingwayapp.com/readability-checker [PRACTITIONER LORE; grade formulas predate screens and are rough]. For Indian audiences reading English as a second language, aim lower (grade 5-7) and shorter sentences.
- Hemingway-style editing: shorten long sentences, prefer active voice, cut adverbs, replace complex words. A mechanical check, not a style.

### 2.4 Feynman technique [PRACTITIONER LORE]
- Explain it as if to a beginner; where you stumble or reach for jargon, you do not understand it yet; go back and simplify. I did not retrieve a primary source in this session; it is widely taught as a study method but is not itself a tested intervention (UNVERIFIED-PRIMARY). Useful here as an audit: if you can only say it in jargon, the draft is not ready.

### 2.5 How great explainers work [PRACTITIONER LORE; from interviews, mostly secondary]
- Kurzgesagt (Philipp Dettmer): aims to trigger curiosity so some viewers dig deeper, not to make viewers experts; wants videos that anyone, including grandparents, can follow; no scientists on the team; sources linked in the description; each video takes months of research and animation. Sources: https://phdnet.mpg.de/208764/episode-25-kurzgesagt-and-visual-science-ft-philipp-dettmer and https://disassociated.com/ten-years-of-kurzgesagt-and-freely-available-quality-content/ (search summaries only).
- 3Blue1Brown (Grant Sanderson): present ideas so viewers feel they could have discovered them; build the tool to fit the idea; videos as "supplemental intuition builder." https://www.stanforddaily.com/2020/01/24/3blue1brown-creator-grant-sanderson-15-talks-engaging-with-math-using-stories-and-visuals and https://www.3blue1brown.com/about (search summaries).
- Cleo Abram (Huge If True): plans the visual before the script ("visual abstract"); looks for a visual that helps viewers understand; picks topics that are inherently visual with a "huge if true" angle; consistent optimistic brand. https://videoconsortium.org/mag/episode-8-cleo-abram and https://podwise.ai/episodes/2768285 (search summaries).
- Common thread: show the thing, one concept, a promise of wonder, depth available but optional. All three work with a visual first.

### 2.6 Writing for the ear (short video) [PRACTITIONER LORE; research basis is modality and redundancy, section 1.4]
- Listeners cannot reread. Short words, short sentences, one thought per breath, repeat the key noun not a pronoun, say numbers as round words ("about 400"). No primary source retrieved for this heading (UNVERIFIED-PRIMARY); the research basis is Mayer's modality, redundancy and voice principles.
- Read the script aloud; if you run out of breath or stumble, cut.

### 2.7 "You" [RESEARCH-BACKED for learning; PRACTITIONER LORE for feed engagement]
- Mayer's personalization principle (d about 0.79, 2017). In feeds, "you" is a signal the post is for the reader.

### 2.8 Metaphors and analogies: when they help, when they hurt [RESEARCH-BACKED, from science education]
- Help: make abstract or unobservable things visualizable and motivate learning (Glynn and Takahashi 1998, cited in review summaries). Hurt: learners map irrelevant features of the analogue onto the target, forming misconceptions; analogies taken too far or applied mechanically reinforce false associations (Harrison and Treagust 2006). The analogue must be familiar to the audience; if it is not, do not use it (Duit 1991; Glynn's Teaching-With-Analogies model). Source: Harrison and Treagust, "Teaching and learning with analogies: friend or foe?" https://acquire.cqu.edu.au/articles/chapter/Teaching_and_learning_with_analogies_friend_or_foe/13440575 (via search). Evidence is from classrooms, not social posts.
- For Tangent: "One sky, two ways to count it" is a metaphor that hides the point. Use an analogy only if it is from everyday life, one sentence, and then state the plain claim.

### Rules for Tangent writers (section 2)
1. Before drafting, write one line: "After this the viewer knows ___." Every slide serves it (Commander's Intent).
2. Order every post: tease / the point / why it matters / optional "go deeper". The first slide alone must make sense (inverted pyramid).
3. Target grade 6-8 English; Indian readers: lean to 5-7. Run a Hemingway-style pass for long sentences and passive voice, but judge by the 12-year-old restate test, not the score.
4. Plan the visual first (what will the viewer SEE?), then write the line that goes with it (Abram).
5. If you can only explain a step with jargon, drop the step or the post (Feynman).
6. Analogy only when drawn from everyday life, then say the literal claim in the next line.
7. For video, read the script aloud, keep sentences breath-length, say "you".

## 3. Attention on mobile feeds

### 3.1 Facebook IQ: fast recall, short dwell [INDUSTRY DATA, platform-run, old]
- Facebook IQ, "Capturing Attention in Feed" (April 2016): Fors Marsh Group tests found it takes 0.25 seconds of exposure for people to recall mobile feed content at a statistically significant rate. People spend about 1.7 seconds with a piece of content on mobile versus 2.5 seconds on desktop. Many viewers scroll with sound off; creative must work without audio. Strongest-recall ads showed the product, delivered a concise message with the right amount of information. https://www.facebook.com/business/news/insights/capturing-attention-feed-video-creative (fetched). Caveat: ad creative from 2014-2015, platform-run, not peer-reviewed, and recall is not comprehension. The 1.7 s figure is for the Facebook feed of that era, not Instagram in 2026.
- Do not claim "viewers decide in 0.25 seconds." The study says they can recall something after 0.25 s, not that they decide then.

### 3.2 NN/g on mobile [RESEARCH-BACKED (usability studies), dated]
- Budiu (19 Apr 2015), "Mobile User Experience: Limitations and Strengths": about 7 years of testing, 151 participants; a 30-inch monitor's above-the-fold content equals about five screenfuls on a 4-inch phone; mobile users rely more on short-term memory; average mobile session 72 seconds versus 150 seconds on desktop. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-ux/ (fetched). The page does not report comprehension percentages. Phones are bigger now; read as direction, not exact figures.
- NN/g (2017) experts crave succinct, scannable text (see 2.3).

### 3.3 Text density and font size on mobile images [PRACTITIONER LORE]
- Vendor blogs converge on body text of at least about 28 px on a 1080 px-wide canvas, larger headlines, high contrast, cut text rather than shrinking it, keep key text out of the bottom 15% where the username overlay sits. Words per slide advice ranges from under 10 to 50. Working target from the sources: about 10-30 words per slide, tested on a phone. Sources: https://postnitro.ai/blog/post/carousel-typography-guide-perfecting-font-sizes-and-spacing and https://adaptlypost.com/blog/text-overlays-on-instagram-carousels (search summary). No official Instagram or peer-reviewed numbers found; units differ between points and pixels across sources.

### 3.4 Attention in short-form video [PRACTITIONER LORE]
- Creator-tool vendors (vidIQ and similar) say the first 1-3 seconds decide the swipe, that creators "re-hook" every few seconds, and that viewers open Shorts to learn something quickly or be entertained and swipe away when it feels like an advert. https://vidiq.com/blog/post/youtube-shorts-algorithm/ (search summary). No official YouTube statement of a threshold found. The 70-90 percent "viewed vs swiped away" figure circulating is anecdotal.

### Rules for Tangent writers (section 3)
1. Slide 1 and the first video second must work with sound off and in a glance. Assume 1-2 seconds of attention, but do not state the 0.25 s figure as a decision time.
2. Large text (at least 28 px body on 1080 px; test on a real phone), high contrast, 10-30 words per slide max, bottom 15 percent kept clear.
3. Captions burned in on all video; the video must make sense muted.
4. Show the thing immediately: the screen recording, the output, the photo, not a title card.
5. Expect interrupted, partial attention: every slide should stand alone.

## 4. Platform mechanics

### 4.1 Instagram ranking signals [OFFICIAL-PLATFORM]
- Adam Mosseri, Jan 2025 video: "The top three signals that matter most for ranking are watch time, likes and sends." Creators should track average watch time, likes per reach and sends per reach in Insights. "Likes are slightly more important for connected content, and sends are slightly more important for unconnected content." Reported by Social Media Today, 22 Jan 2025: https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-shares-algorithm-insights-2025/738034/ (fetched). Claims that sends are weighted "3-5x" likes are not in his words (Funnl, unsupported); do not repeat.
- Implication: simple, clear and shareable posts ("send this to a friend who...") earn sends, and sends are most valuable for reaching non-followers, which Tangent needs.
- Tangent's pages also say Instagram is Mosseri's priority for "original" content; I did not verify that; omitted.

### 4.2 Carousel second chance [OFFICIAL-PLATFORM, relayed]
- Mosseri (reported 17 Oct 2024): "if someone sees your carousel post but they don't swipe, we'll often give that carousel a second chance," which can mean showing the next slide in feed. https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/ig-chief-recommends-posting-carousels-to-improve-reach/730232/ (fetched). The page quotes him via a post, not a primary link.
- Consequence: slide 2 can be the first thing a person sees. Slide 2 must also hook, and the carousel must not depend on slide 1 context to make sense.

### 4.3 Limits and ratios [OFFICIAL-PLATFORM claims reported by trade press; details UNVERIFIED-PRIMARY]
- 20 slides: Instagram raised the carousel limit from 10 to 20 in Aug 2024, rolling out gradually. Via https://www.online-tech-tips.com/?p=102378 and https://vistasocial.com/insights/instagram-carousel-limit-doubled-to-20-photos-or-videos/ (secondary; no Instagram page retrieved).
- Grid and ratios: third-party guides say the profile grid moved to 3:4 in 2025 and native 3:4 uploads arrived around May 2025; 4:5 (1080x1350) remains the common default, and a 4:5 post loses a thin strip at the sides in the 3:4 grid. Guides disagree on dates and API support. Sources: https://nealschaffer.com/instagram-post-size/ and https://influencermarketinghub.com/?p=20584 . I did not find Instagram's own announcement. Safe design: use 4:5 or 3:4 with all text inside a central safe area so either crop keeps it.
- Captions truncation, links not clickable in captions, alt text: my search did not reach Instagram's Help Center for truncation or links (UNVERIFIED-PRIMARY). Alt text editing after posting is documented at https://help.instagram.com/iphone-app/503708446705527 (appeared in search; I did not fetch it). Practitioner advice: the first line of a caption is what shows before "more", so put the point there; use "link in bio" rather than a URL.
- Trial Reels: test a Reel with non-followers before showing followers; metrics after about 24 hours; launched late 2024 (one source says 10 Dec 2024); later opened to public accounts with 1,000+ followers and scheduling added. https://www.socialsamosa.com/news-2/instagram-introduces-wider-access-trial-reels-9493132 and https://socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-expands-access-trial-reels/752895/ (search summaries). Check Tangent's follower count against the 1,000 threshold; it is a free A/B test for hook clarity.

### 4.4 YouTube Shorts [OFFICIAL-PLATFORM for definitions; hook advice is PRACTITIONER LORE]
- YouTube Studio's "Viewed vs. swiped away" is the share of times viewers watched a Short versus swiped away; the Help page I fetched (https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12220281) describes "How viewers engaged" as "the percentage of times viewers stayed to watch past the initial seconds of a Short," and gives no hook guidance. YouTube does not publish a ranking weight or a good/bad threshold. I could not retrieve Creator Insider or YouTube blog posts in this session, so the official line that Shorts leads people to long-form is not verified here.
- Practical lore (vidIQ, Buffer, Outlier): a clear topic and promise in the first 1-3 seconds, a loop-friendly ending, and a specific, plainly named topic.

### Rules for Tangent writers (section 4)
1. Optimise for the three signals Mosseri named: watch time, likes per reach, sends per reach. Make something worth sending: one clear, surprising, useful thing.
2. Slide 2 must be able to start the carousel (second-chance behaviour). Test every slide cold.
3. Put the point in the first caption line; no URL in captions; "link in bio" for depth.
4. Use 4:5 (or 3:4) with all text inside a central safe margin so both grid crops work.
5. Use Trial Reels (if eligible) to test two hook wordings before posting to followers.
6. Judge Shorts by "viewed vs swiped away" and average view duration; if viewed share is low, rewrite the first line, not the body.

## 5. Carousel best practice

### 5.1 Carousels versus Reels [INDUSTRY DATA]
- Socialinsider, Instagram benchmarks (2025 data, page presented as 2026): engagement rate carousels 0.55%, Reels 0.52%, images 0.37%; sample 67.5 million posts from 579,000+ business accounts of 1K-1M followers. Average views per post for 100K-1M follower accounts: carousels 35,370, images 22,900, Reels 16,035. https://www.socialinsider.io/social-media-benchmarks/instagram (fetched). Another summary cites an earlier edition with 35 million posts; sample size reporting is inconsistent, and the page also shows quarterly 2026 numbers (Q3: carousels 0.50%, Reels 0.48%), so treat decimals as provisional. Brand accounts, not solo creators. Engagement rate definitions differ across vendors.

### 5.2 Slide count [INDUSTRY DATA; conflicting]
- Socialinsider (2020, about 3 million carousels): 2-slide carousels averaged 1.90% engagement and 8+ slides did better still; the middle (5-6) was lowest. https://www.socialinsider.io/data-geeks/Instagram-Carousel-Study.pdf (via search summary).
- PostEverywhere (2026, its own customers' posts): 7-10 slides beat the account's norm by 17%, 4-6 by 13%, 2-3 by 5% (small sample for 2-3). https://posteverywhere.ai/blog/instagram-carousel-best-practices (search summary).
- Socialinsider's current page gives no best count; advises longer carousels only when valuable enough to save.
- Conclusion: no reliable optimum. For Tangent: as many slides as the single idea needs, which for a one-idea post is usually 4-7. Fewer, stronger slides beat padded ones given the cognitive-load evidence. Do not pad to hit 8.

### 5.3 Hook slide, swipe momentum, CTA, template [PRACTITIONER LORE]
- Hook slide: plain statement of the promise or a concrete surprise; no riddles. Momentum: each slide ends with an open question that the next answers (Loewenstein gap, section 1.7). Last slide: one CTA only (send, save, or follow for part 2). Consistent template (same fonts, colors, positions) reduces extraneous load because the viewer learns where to look (spatial contiguity, signaling). These are expert conventions; none of the vendor sources I retrieved tested them experimentally.

### Rules for Tangent writers (section 5)
1. One idea per carousel. 4-7 slides. Never pad.
2. Slide 1 = the promise in plain words plus the image of the thing. Slide 2 stands alone.
3. Each slide ends on a small open question the next slide answers.
4. Last slide: one CTA ("send this to someone who...") not three.
5. One fixed template; vary only the image and the line.
6. Track sends per reach and saves per post, not just likes.

## 6. The audiences

### 6.1 General public attitudes to AI [RESEARCH-BACKED]
- Pew (June 9-15, 2025; n=5,023 US adults): 50% more concerned than excited, 10% more excited, 38% equally both; concern up from 37% in 2021; 57% rate societal risks high versus 25% who rate benefits high; about half expect AI to worsen creative thinking and relationships. Pew's Mar 12 2026 key-findings page: concern is lower in many of the other 24 countries polled; medical care the most optimistic domain (44% positive over 20 years versus 19% negative, Aug 2024); weather and data analysis welcomed; younger adults slightly more excited; 47% heard a lot about AI. https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/03/12/key-findings-about-how-americans-view-artificial-intelligence/ (fetched) and https://www.pewresearch.org/science/2025/09/17/how-americans-view-ai (search listing). Pew's global median: 34% more concerned than excited.
- Implication: the global feed audience is wary. A post about useful, specific, personal things AI does (a photo fixed, a question answered) lands better than abstract "AI is amazing" claims. A post about AI replacing creativity touches the biggest worry.

### 6.2 India [RESEARCH-BACKED, with a correction to the premise]
- Ipsos AI Monitor 2025 (30 countries; India fieldwork 21 Mar-4 Apr 2025): Indians were both excited (65%) and nervous (66%); excitement is "at par" with Asia-Pacific peers, not uniquely high. 67% want products to disclose AI use; Indians trust AI-made company content more than the global average (47% vs 34% for enhanced product images); more prefer human-made news (46% vs 40%) and advertising (47% vs 40%); expected benefits in the next 3-5 years: jobs 55%, time saved 51%, health 50%, entertainment 50%; 49% expect more disinformation. https://www.ipsos.com/en-in/trust-and-transparency-emerge-key-cornerstones-ai-usage-among-indians-ipsos-ai-monitor-2025 (fetched). Reports that India "leads the world in optimism" come from secondary coverage (a claim attributed to Stanford HAI's AI Index) that I could not confirm; the primary Ipsos data show mixed feelings, high excitement and high nervousness together.
- Implication: Indian viewers are open to practical, time-saving, health, learning uses, and also care about trust and honesty. Disclosing that the app is AI and showing it being checked builds trust. Panditji-style cultural topics should be treated with respect and plain claims.

### 6.3 What curious-but-non-technical audiences respond to [PRACTITIONER LORE]
- Practical everyday benefit, wonder ("huge if true"), a story about a person. Evidence: Cleo Abram's optimistic, visual, benefit-led format; Kurzgesagt's goal of triggering curiosity; Pew's finding that people are most positive about concrete helpful uses (medical, weather) and least positive about abstract claims about creativity and jobs. Qualitative inference rather than a direct test.

### 6.4 What tech enthusiasts want, and the two-layer design [PRACTITIONER LORE built on research-backed elements]
- NN/g: even expert readers want succinct, scannable text and reject jargon (2017). So enthusiasts do not need density on the feed; they need a path to depth.
- Two-layer design = inverted pyramid applied to social: Layer 1 (everyone): one plain-English idea per slide that completes the point by itself. Layer 2 (enthusiasts): an optional hook on the last slide or caption: "Want to know how I stopped the AI doing the maths? Link in bio." The depth lives off-feed (a pinned comment, link in bio, long-form video, written article), never in the slides.
- Kurzgesagt's "triggers interest, links sources in the description" and Axios's "go deeper" are the same pattern.

### Rules for Tangent writers (section 6)
1. Assume a wary, non-technical viewer. Lead with the benefit or the wonder as a person's story ("I used AI to fix my dad's old photo").
2. Show what the AI did; do not argue that AI is good.
3. Disclose plainly that AI is involved and show one honest check (builds trust, matters for India).
4. Slides carry the whole story. Enthusiast depth (method, tests, code) goes behind one "link in bio" or "ask me how" line, never on the slides.
5. Never put the same-weight technical term on a slide "because enthusiasts like it"; they too prefer scannable.

## 8. Consolidated rulebook, ranked by evidence strength

### Tier A: RESEARCH-BACKED (apply always)
1. Make text easy to process; hard words and hard fonts lower perceived intelligence and trust. (Oppenheimer 2006; Reber et al. 2004; Alter and Oppenheimer 2009)
2. One idea per slide; remove everything that does not serve it (Mayer coherence d about 0.70-0.86; cognitive load theory).
3. Words plus a picture of the thing on every slide; label on the object (multimedia, spatial contiguity, d about 0.79; dual coding).
4. Concrete over abstract: name the thing, number, person, picture (concreteness effect, about 2:1 memory advantage in Paivio's summary).
5. Do not repeat the same words in two places (redundancy d about 0.87); in video, voice carries the explanation, text is a few words.
6. Write conversationally with "you" and "I" in a human voice (personalization d about 0.79; voice d about 0.74).
7. Define a term before using it or avoid it (pre-training d about 0.46); you are a curse-of-knowledge risk, so test every slide on an outsider.
8. Chunk to the limit: a slide holds about 3-4 things at most (Cowan).
9. Use an analogy only if familiar and then state the plain claim (analogy research).
10. Experts also prefer plain, scannable text (NN/g 2017); so plain does not cost you the enthusiasts.
11. Curiosity: a small, closeable gap about something the viewer already pictures (Loewenstein 1994).

### Tier B: OFFICIAL-PLATFORM
12. Optimise watch time, likes per reach, sends per reach (Mosseri, Jan 2025); sends matter most for reaching new people.
13. Unswiped carousels may re-appear starting at slide 2; every slide must work cold (Mosseri, Oct 2024).
14. Carousels may be up to 20 slides but length is not a goal.
15. Shorts: watch "viewed vs swiped away" and average view duration; no official threshold exists.
16. Trial Reels (1,000+ followers) can test hook clarity on non-followers.

### Tier C: INDUSTRY DATA
17. Carousels edge Reels on engagement rate (0.55% vs 0.52%, 2025 brand data, Socialinsider) and on views per post at larger account sizes; images lag. Do not over-read decimals.
18. No reliable best slide count; vendor data conflict. Use only as many slides as the idea needs (about 4-7).
19. Feed dwell is short (about 1.7 s on mobile in Facebook's 2016 data); design for a glance and sound off, with caveat on age and platform.
20. Phone-sized, high-contrast text: body at least about 28 px on 1080 px, 10-30 words per slide, bottom 15% clear.

### Tier D: PRACTITIONER LORE
21. Write the one-line intent first (Made to Stick); order tease, point, why it matters, go deeper (Smart Brevity, inverted pyramid).
22. Plan the visual before the line (Abram); show the thing in the first second.
23. Slide 1 = promise in plain words; each slide ends on a small question; final slide has one CTA; one fixed template.
24. Two-layer design: slides complete for everyone; depth behind one "link in bio" or "ask me how".
25. Grade 6-8 English (5-7 for Indian second-language readers); read scripts aloud; Hemingway-style pass, but judge by the 12-year-old restate test.
26. Lead with benefit, wonder and a person; disclose AI use and show one honest check (Pew, Ipsos).
