# NLP (Bandler and Grinder) and plain social-media writing

Research date: 2026-11-15. Scope: Neuro-Linguistic Programming (the Bandler/Grinder school), NOT natural language processing.
Purpose: decide which NLP-derived ideas, if any, help Tangent write short, simple posts (carousels, captions, reels, Shorts) that people understand and engage with.

## Evidence tags used in this file

- **[RESEARCH-BACKED]**: supported by mainstream peer-reviewed research (citation given).
- **[NLP-CLAIM, UNSUPPORTED]**: a claim NLP makes that is contradicted, untested, or unsupported.
- **[CRAFT HEURISTIC]**: a writing rule of thumb with no strong evidence either way, kept because it is cheap and low-risk.
- **[INFERENCE]**: my reasoning applying the above to Tangent's situation. Not tested on Instagram. Treat as a hypothesis to check against real post data.

Citation honesty: sources marked "(verified)" were opened or confirmed by search results this session. Sources marked "(from knowledge)" are well-known papers I cite from memory without a re-check this session; check volume/page details before quoting them in public. Several verified items come only from abstracts and secondary summaries, and I say so where it matters.

# PART 1: THE RESEARCH

## 1.0 What NLP is, in one paragraph

NLP was created in the early-to-mid 1970s at UC Santa Cruz by Richard Bandler (a student) and John Grinder (a linguist). Its founding text is *The Structure of Magic, Vol. I* (Science and Behavior Books, 1975), which proposed the Meta Model, a set of language patterns for therapists, borrowed in vocabulary (surface structure, deep structure, deletion) from Chomsky's transformational grammar. Later books added the Milton Model (*Patterns of the Hypnotic Techniques of Milton H. Erickson, M.D.*, Vol. I 1975, Vol. II 1977), representational systems (*Frogs into Princes*, 1979), and so on. Linguists point out that beyond borrowed terminology NLP does not resemble Chomsky's actual theory (Stollznow, quoted in the [Wikipedia article on Grinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Grinder) (verified, secondary source)).

- Some secondary sources cite Platt (2003) as finding about one third of studies supportive of representational-system claims; I could not verify this. Treat as a minority position, not a rebuttal.
- Mainstream psychology also contains discredited and half-replicated findings (see fluency caveats in 1.14), so "mainstream" is not a guarantee either. Where I use a mainstream finding I note replication caveats.

**What this means for Tangent.** Do not cite NLP to the audience, do not claim NLP works, and do not use NLP technique names as authority. Use the underlying ideas only where they match well-studied mainstream effects or are plain low-risk craft advice. [INFERENCE]

## 1.2 The Meta Model

**Definition.** A set of questions and a taxonomy of how everyday speech omits, alters, or over-generalises the speaker's actual experience. Speakers delete information, distort it, and generalise it; the listener "recovers" the lost material by asking specific questions ("Who specifically?", "How, specifically?", "Compared to what?"). Canonical source: Bandler and Grinder, *The Structure of Magic, Vol. I* (1975). Background on the link to transformational grammar: [Grinder, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Grinder) (verified, secondary).

**The patterns (the standard classroom list; exact labels vary slightly by trainer):**

| Category | Pattern | Example | Recovery question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deletion | Unspecified noun | "They don't listen." | Who, specifically? |
| Deletion | Unspecified verb | "He rejected me." | How, specifically? |
| Deletion | Comparative deletion | "This is better." | Better than what? |
| Deletion | Lost performative (value judgment with the judge removed) | "It's wrong to complain." | Who says? According to whom? |
| Generalisation | Universal quantifiers | "Nobody ever helps." | Never? Nobody? |
| Generalisation | Modal operators (necessity, possibility) | "I can't." / "I must." | What stops you? What would happen if you did? |
| Distortion | Nominalisation (a process frozen into a noun) | "Our communication is poor." | Who is communicating what to whom, and how? |
| Distortion | Mind reading | "He doesn't like me." | How do you know? |
| Distortion | Cause-effect | "You make me angry." | How does X cause Y? |
| Distortion | Complex equivalence (two things treated as the same) | "She's not smiling, so she hates it." | How does X mean Y? |
| Distortion | Presupposition | "Why won't you accept help?" | What makes you think I won't? |

**How it works in language.** Every utterance is a compression of a richer experience. Compression is normal and necessary. The model gives a systematic way to see what was compressed away.

**Evidence status.**
- As a therapy technique: [NLP-CLAIM, UNSUPPORTED]. Sturt (2012) and Witkowski (2010) above find no robust effect of NLP-based interventions. The claim that recovering deleted information "changes the client's model of the world" is untested as stated.
- As a *description of language*: the patterns are old, recognisable linguistic and logical phenomena (ellipsis, nominalisation, quantification, presupposition, modality). That they exist is not in dispute and does not need NLP. The tidy deletion/distortion/generalisation scheme is not a standard linguistic taxonomy. [CRAFT HEURISTIC]
- As a *writer's diagnostic*: no one has tested "Meta Model editing improves social posts." But the diagnostic reduces to this question: **can the reader reconstruct the specific who/what/how without effort?** That maps onto well-supported mainstream findings (fluency, concreteness, curse of knowledge, presupposition; sections 1.14 and 2.x). So the diagnostic is useful as a checklist even though the NLP therapy claims are not. [INFERENCE]

**Why this matters most for Tangent.** The five failed lines are almost textbook Meta Model violations (Part 2).

## 1.3 The Milton Model

**Definition.** The Meta Model's "mirror image": deliberately vague, "artfully vague" language patterns attributed to the hypnotist Milton Erickson, meant to let the listener fill in the blanks from their own experience and so enter a receptive or trance-like state. Canonical source: Bandler and Grinder, *Patterns of the Hypnotic Techniques of Milton H. Erickson, M.D.*, Vol. I (1975); Grinder, DeLozier and Bandler, Vol. II (1977) (from knowledge). Typical patterns: nominalisations, unspecified verbs, universal quantifiers, mind reading ("you are probably wondering..."), embedded commands, presuppositions, tag questions, ambiguity, "cause-effect" bridges ("as you read this, you will relax").

**How it works in language.** Deleted or vague referents force the listener to supply their own content. In one-to-one hypnosis the listener is cooperative, motivated and attending, so the filled-in content feels personal.

**Evidence status.**
- Indirect/permissive (Ericksonian) wording versus direct wording: reviews find suggestion style has little effect on objective hypnotic responding, and the better-controlled studies do not show indirect suggestion to be superior. See Lynn, Neufeld and Maré (1993), ["Direct versus indirect suggestions: a conceptual and methodological review"](https://scilit.net/publications/61ec1e9751df71f511cce308d5b91850) (verified abstract only) and the Bayesian reanalysis in Martin and Dienes (2019), [PDF](https://users.sussex.ac.uk/~dienes/Martin%20and%20Dienes%202019%20Bayes%20to%20the%20rescue.pdf) (verified; shows that such comparisons typically find no significant differences and calls for more research). So the claim that artful vagueness has special power: [NLP-CLAIM, UNSUPPORTED].
- What IS well supported: vague, generic personality descriptions feel personally accurate to the person reading them. That is the Barnum/Forer effect (Forer 1949, from knowledge), the mechanism behind astrology and horoscope acceptance. Milton Model patterns therefore resemble Barnum statements. **This matters directly because Panditji is an astrology guide.** See ethics (1.17).

**Is the "AI voice" an accidental Milton Model?** See 2.2. Short answer: it has the vagueness of one and none of the conditions that make vagueness work. [INFERENCE]

## 1.4 Representational systems (VAK) and sensory predicates

**Definition.** The claim that people think in visual, auditory, kinaesthetic (and olfactory/gustatory) "representational systems", that each person has a preferred one, that it is revealed by their sensory predicates ("I see what you mean" vs "I hear you" vs "it feels right") and by eye-movement direction (eye accessing cues), and that matching a listener's predicates builds rapport and persuades. Canonical sources: Bandler and Grinder, *Frogs into Princes* (1979); Dilts, Grinder, Bandler and DeLozier, *Neuro-Linguistic Programming, Vol. I* (1980) (from knowledge).

**Evidence status, split into three separate claims (do not merge them):**
1. *People have a preferred system detectable from predicates, and matching it improves rapport:* [NLP-CLAIM, UNSUPPORTED]. This is exactly what Sharpley (1984, 1987) and Heap (1988, 2008) reviewed and found lacking.
2. *Eye movements reveal sensory modality or lying:* [NLP-CLAIM, UNSUPPORTED], and directly tested: [Wiseman et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3394779) found nothing across three studies, including real press-conference footage.
3. *Concrete, sensory-rich language is easier to process and remember than abstract language:* [RESEARCH-BACKED], under another name: the concreteness effect and dual coding theory (Paivio; Paivio 1991 *Canadian J. Psychology* 45:255-287, from knowledge). Concrete words are processed faster and remembered better; the account is that they engage both a verbal and an imagery system. A 1994 ERP study supports the dual-coding account over the main rival (context availability): Kounios and Holcomb, ["Concreteness effects in semantic processing: ERP evidence supporting dual-coding theory"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8064248/) (verified). In marketing: Packard and Berger found that concrete language used by customer-service agents raised customer satisfaction and purchase intent ([Wharton summary](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/how-language-boosts-customer-satisfaction/); verified secondary; published in the *Journal of Consumer Research*; I did not read the paper).

**Important nuance, a counter-example to the "sensory is always better" folk belief.** An analysis of 447,792 Amazon reviews found sensory reviews were *less* likely to be rated helpful than non-sensory ones, because readers saw sensory accounts as less objective (Lopez and Garza 2022, [record](https://www.econbiz.de/Record/do-sensory-reviews-make-more-sense-the-mediation-of-objective-perception-in-online-review-helpfulness/10012812664); verified abstract-level only). Concrete is not the same as sensory-flowery. A concrete *fact* ("it gave a different Saturn position every time I asked") beats a sensory *decoration* ("the sky shimmered with answers"). So: use concrete nouns and checkable specifics, not ornament. [INFERENCE from the two findings]

**Verdict for writers.** Drop "match their VAK type" entirely. Keep "use concrete, picturable words." [RESEARCH-BACKED for the second; the first is unsupported.]

## 1.5 Pacing and leading; rapport; matching the audience's map

**Definition.** *Pacing*: first describing the listener's present experience, belief or words so accurately that they agree ("yes, that's me"). *Leading*: then introducing something new, in small steps, while rapport holds. *Rapport*: the felt sense of being understood, supposedly built by matching posture, breathing, tempo, predicates, and values. "The map is not the territory" (Korzybski) is borrowed as the idea that each audience has its own "map" and you should speak in it. Sources: Bandler and Grinder (1979) *Frogs into Princes*; Erickson's practice (from knowledge).

**Evidence status.**
- *Nonverbal matching builds rapport by a trick of technique:* mixed. Unconscious mimicry does correlate with liking, and being mimicked can increase liking and smoothness of interaction (Chartrand and Bargh 1999, [abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10402679/), verified), but that paper shows natural, nonconscious mimicry, not a trainable persuasion technique; the secondary literature I found notes the rapport/trust mediation came from *strategic* mimicry in one study. So: [RESEARCH-BACKED] that people like those who mirror them; [NLP-CLAIM, UNSUPPORTED] that a deliberate NLP matching protocol produces measurable persuasion. Not applicable to text or video posts in any case.
- *Matching language style:* there is research on "language style matching" in conversations (Ireland et al. 2011, *Psychological Science*, from knowledge). Applicability to a one-to-many post is unclear. [INFERENCE]
- *Pacing-then-leading as a rhetorical order* (start where the reader is, then move them): not tested as NLP, but it is the same structure as every good explanation: start from what the audience already believes or has experienced, then add one new thing. Related mainstream ideas: prior knowledge predicts comprehension; the curse of knowledge (1.15). [CRAFT HEURISTIC]

**Application.** Pace = open with the reader's own situation or a belief they already hold, stated in words they would use ("You ask ChatGPT where Saturn is. It tells you. You ask again. A different answer."). Lead = the single new idea. Do not use it to manufacture agreement the reader has not actually given (see "yes set" and ethics).

## 1.6 Presuppositions

NLP uses the word in two ways, and they must not be confused.

**(a) The "NLP presuppositions"** (operating beliefs such as "the map is not the territory", "people make the best choices available to them", "there is no failure, only feedback"). These are attitudes, not testable claims. [CRAFT HEURISTIC] at best; some are plainly false as stated.

**(b) Linguistic presuppositions**: assumptions built into a sentence's grammar that the listener accepts without noticing ("When you try the app" presupposes you will; "Why is it so inaccurate?" presupposes it is). Canonical NLP source: Bandler and Grinder (1975, 1976); the linguistic phenomenon itself is standard semantics/pragmatics.

**Evidence status for (b): [RESEARCH-BACKED], under another name.** Loftus and Palmer (1974), ["Reconstruction of automobile destruction"](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/cognitive-bias/1974-loftus.pdf), *J. Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior* 13:585-589 (verified citation; full text not read here): changing one verb in a question ("smashed" vs "hit") changed estimated speed and, a week later, the chance of falsely recalling broken glass. Loftus (1975), ["Leading questions and the eyewitness report"](https://app.nova.edu/toolbox/instructionalproducts/edd8124/articles/1975-Loftus.pdf), *Cognitive Psychology* 7:560-572 (verified; fragmentary text): asking about "*the* broken headlight" rather than "*a* broken headlight" made witnesses more likely to report one that did not exist. Presupposition (the definite article, the loaded verb) steers what people accept as given. Caveat: lab settings, and later work has qualified how far misinformation effects generalise.

**Two uses in writing, one good and one bad.**
- *Failure mode:* the definite article presupposes a shared referent the reader does not have. "The maths", "the model", "the sky's reference books": each says "you know which one I mean." They don't. This is a Loftus-style presupposition failing in the opposite direction: it does not plant a false belief, it plants a missing referent and the reader has to guess. [INFERENCE]
- *Ethics:* using presupposition to smuggle in a claim you could not defend directly ("When Panditji gets your chart right...") is manipulative. State claims openly and let readers evaluate them.

## 1.7 Chunking up, down and sideways; level of abstraction

**Definition.** *Chunking up*: moving to a more general, abstract category ("a car" -> "transport"). *Chunking down*: moving to a more specific example ("transport" -> "my 2012 Honda Civic"). *Chunking sideways*: moving to a parallel instance at the same level (analogy, "what else is like this?"). Sources: Dilts, Hall and others; the idea of levels of abstraction predates NLP (Hayakawa's "ladder of abstraction" in *Language in Thought and Action*, 1941, from knowledge).

**Evidence status: [RESEARCH-BACKED], as construal level theory.** Trope and Liberman (2010), ["Construal-level theory of psychological distance"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20438233/), *Psychological Review* 117(2):440-463 (verified abstract; [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3152826)): the further something is from the here-and-now (in time, space, social distance, or hypotheticality), the more abstractly people mentally represent it. The reverse also holds: near things are represented in concrete detail. Abstract framing suits distant, strategic thinking ("why"); concrete framing suits near, practical doing ("how"). Replication caveat: CLT is a large literature with some contested moderators; the general direction is well supported but effect sizes vary. (from knowledge)

**Why it matters for posts.** A scroll is a near-term, low-effort, concrete moment. Abstract framing at the point of a hook asks the reader to do the abstraction work; the audience cannot picture anything. The usual fix: chunk *down* to one specific example, then (only if needed) chunk up in one plain sentence. The failed lines are chunked up without ever coming down (see Part 2). [INFERENCE]

## 1.8 Reframing (context and content)

**Definition.** Changing the meaning of an event by changing its frame. *Content reframing*: "what else could this mean?" ("He's stubborn" -> "He's persistent"). *Context reframing*: "where would this be useful?" Canonical source: Bandler and Grinder, *Reframing: Neuro-Linguistic Programming and the Transformation of Meaning* (1982) (from knowledge).

**Evidence status: [RESEARCH-BACKED] under other names.**
- *Framing effects*: Tversky and Kahneman (1981), ["The framing of decisions and the psychology of choice"](https://www.csc2.ncsu.edu/faculty/mpsingh/local/Social/f24/wrap/readings/Tversky+Kahneman-framing-1981.pdf), *Science* 211:453-458 (verified): the same outcomes, described as "200 saved" vs "400 die", reversed preferences. Wording of identical facts changes responses.
- *Metaphor framing*: Thibodeau and Boroditsky (2011), "Metaphors we think with" [doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0016782](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0016782), *PLoS ONE* 6(2):e16782 (verified via secondary coverage): crime described as a "beast" vs a "virus" shifted preferred policy, and participants rarely credited the metaphor. Note the implication for ethics: framing works even when readers don't notice it, which is why it must be used openly and honestly.
- *Cognitive reappraisal* in emotion regulation (Gross, e.g. 1998, from knowledge) is the clinical cousin of "reframing".

**Application.** Honest reframing is a fine post shape: "Everyone assumes the AI is the clever part. In this app, it's the least clever part. The clever part is a free planet-position table." Use a reframe only if you can show the audience the evidence. [CRAFT HEURISTIC]

## 1.9 Anchoring

**Definition (NLP).** Linking a stimulus (a touch, word, gesture, image) to an emotional state so that firing the stimulus later re-triggers the state. Sources: Bandler and Grinder, various; Pavlov is the unacknowledged ancestor.

**Evidence status.**
- *As NLP "anchoring" practised in coaching:* [NLP-CLAIM, UNSUPPORTED] as a distinct, reliable technique.
- *The same idea under another name:* classical/evaluative conditioning, and repetition-based association (e.g. a recurring sound or visual signature on a channel becomes tied to the content). [RESEARCH-BACKED] in general; not specific to NLP. (from knowledge)
- **Name collision warning:** the *anchoring heuristic* in judgment (Tversky and Kahneman 1974: a first number biases later estimates) is a different thing from NLP anchoring. Do not mix them.

**Application.** For a creator: a consistent opening format, a recurring phrase, a recurring visual element become associated with the brand. That is branding, not hypnosis. [CRAFT HEURISTIC] Using music or visuals to *induce an emotion the content hasn't earned* is manipulative.

## 1.10 Future pacing

**Definition.** Mentally rehearsing the desired outcome in a future context so the new behaviour "carries over", usually imagining, in sensory detail, yourself using it. Sources: NLP texts, practitioner manuals.

**Evidence status: [RESEARCH-BACKED] under other names.**
- *Implementation intentions* ("if X happens, I will do Y"): Gollwitzer (1999), ["Implementation intentions: strong effects of simple plans"](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/99_Gollwitzer_Implementation_Intentions.pdf), *American Psychologist* 54(7):493-503 (verified). A later review reported medium-to-large effects on self-regulation (d around 0.6-0.8; figures from a secondary summary I did not verify).
- *Process-focused mental simulation* (imagining the steps) outperforms purely outcome-focused fantasy: Pham and Taylor (1999, *PSPB*) and Oettingen's work on mental contrasting (Oettingen and Mayer 2002) (both from knowledge). Caution: purely positive fantasising about the outcome can *lower* effort.
So future pacing is supported only in its "specific if-then plan / imagined next step" form.

**Application to a call to action.** A CTA that asks the reader to picture a small concrete next step ("Tonight, when you open your photo app, try this one slider") is stronger than "Try this!" or a vague aspiration. It also respects the reader. [INFERENCE from Gollwitzer; not tested on social posts.]

## 1.11 Embedded commands and the "yes set"

**Embedded commands.** Commands hidden inside a longer sentence, marked by voice tone, so that the listener's unconscious responds ("I don't know if you will *buy this now* or later"). Source: Bandler and Grinder's Milton patterns; Ericksonian practice.
- Evidence: [NLP-CLAIM, UNSUPPORTED]. I found no controlled evidence that embedded commands cause compliance, and the indirect-suggestion literature above (Lynn et al. 1993; Martin and Dienes 2019) gives no support for the broader claim. Relatedly, psychological reactance research (Brehm 1966, from knowledge) suggests that perceived manipulation or pressure can *backfire*.
- Ethics: **do not use.** The design intent is to bypass conscious evaluation. That is incompatible with Tangent's honesty and AI-disclosure brand.

**Yes set / yes ladder.** Getting a run of easy "yes" answers so that the listener is more likely to say yes to the real request.
- Evidence: mixed and mostly by analogy. Related mainstream effects: consistency/commitment and foot-in-the-door (Freedman and Fraser 1966, from knowledge), which are real but modest and context-dependent. A rapid "yes, yes, yes" sequence in a one-way post has no real "yes" to elicit. [CRAFT HEURISTIC] at most.
- Honest version of the idea: start from facts the reader can verify themselves ("Photo apps have 40 sliders. You use 3.") and build to your point. That is just a clear argument. The *manipulative* version, in which every statement is engineered to be true-but-irrelevant so you drift into agreeing with a false conclusion, must not be used.

## 1.12 Well-formed outcomes

**Definition.** A checklist for goals: stated positively, specific, sensory-grounded ("how will you know?"), within your control, ecologically fine (consequences considered), with resources identified. Source: NLP trainers (Dilts, Andreas and others); a sibling of SMART goals.

**Evidence status.** As NLP: no strong evidence either way. Under another name, goal-setting research is among the most robust in applied psychology (Locke and Latham 2002, *American Psychologist* 57:705-717, from knowledge): specific, challenging goals beat vague "do your best" goals. [RESEARCH-BACKED] for "specific beats vague".

**Application to posts.** Before writing, state the post's outcome in one sentence with an observable test: "After this carousel, a general reader can say what Panditji does in one sentence" or "a tech reader taps 'more' because they want to know how the maths was moved out of the model." If you cannot say it, the post has no well-formed outcome and the slides will wander. [CRAFT HEURISTIC]

## 1.13 Submodalities

**Definition.** The fine qualities of an inner image, sound or feeling (brightness, size, distance, colour, location, volume). NLP claims that changing them changes the emotional charge or belief attached to a memory ("make the picture smaller and dimmer to reduce craving"). Source: Bandler, *Using Your Brain for a Change* (1985) (from knowledge).

**Evidence status.**
- NLP's specific change-the-submodality techniques: [NLP-CLAIM, UNSUPPORTED] as a general method; poorly tested.
- Related well-supported science: mental imagery vividness shapes emotional response, and imagery is central in memory, anxiety and motivation (Holmes and Mathews 2010, *Clinical Psychology Review* 30:349-362, from knowledge). [RESEARCH-BACKED] that imagery matters; unsupported that NLP's dial-turning recipes are the way to use it.

**Application.** Not a writing tool. The visual cousin is simple: one clear, high-contrast image per slide. [CRAFT HEURISTIC]

## 1.14 Perceptual positions

**Definition.** Looking at a situation from the first position (your own view), the second (the other person's) and the third (an uninvolved observer). Source: Grinder and DeLozier, *Turtles All the Way Down* (1987); Dilts (from knowledge).

**Evidence status.** Perspective-taking itself is well studied (e.g. Galinsky and Moskowitz 2000, from knowledge). The idea that deliberate "second position" exercises fix communication problems is [CRAFT HEURISTIC]. It is nonetheless the **direct antidote to the curse of knowledge**: Newton's tapping study (1990) found tappers predicted listeners would name about 50% of songs and listeners actually named about 2.5% (see secondary summary, [Decision Lab](https://www.thedecisionlab.com/reference-guide/management/curse-of-knowledge); original: Newton 1990 Stanford dissertation; term coined in Camerer, Loewenstein and Weber 1989, *J. Political Economy*; verified via secondary sources only). [RESEARCH-BACKED] that experts cannot easily un-know what they know.

**Application.** The writer is in first position (knows Saturn kept moving). The scroller is in second (knows none of it). A pre-publish check: read each slide as someone who has never seen the app. [CRAFT HEURISTIC, grounded in RESEARCH-BACKED curse of knowledge]

## 1.15 Dilts' logical levels (neurological levels)

**Definition.** Robert Dilts' hierarchy of environment, behaviour, capability, beliefs/values, identity, (purpose/spirituality), adapted from Gregory Bateson's logical levels of learning. Claim: change at a higher level cascades down; problems are often located at one level but "solved" at another. Sources: Dilts (1990), *Changing Belief Systems with NLP*; Bateson (1972), *Steps to an Ecology of Mind* (from knowledge).

**Evidence status: [NLP-CLAIM, UNSUPPORTED] as a hierarchy.** I found no empirical test of the model. Critiques noted a lack of ordering principle and the under-substantiated "neurological" label; the best of these were low-authority sources (blogs, a Scribd copy), so I flag them as leads, not findings. A Surrey-repository abstract also lists criticism of Dilts' levels within a broader theoretical critique. The model is also commonly criticised for the claimed Bateson link.

**As a craft heuristic: [CRAFT HEURISTIC].** The six labels are a handy way to notice which *level* a sentence speaks at. For a general audience, the engaging level is usually **environment and behaviour** (what happened, what the thing does) with a hint of **capability** ("you can"). Identity-level lines ("You're the kind of person who...") feel like tribal flattery and drift toward Barnum statements. Method-level lines ("The model only explains it") are the tech-enthusiast depth layer and belong behind the "more" tap. [INFERENCE]

## 1.16 Metaphor and storytelling in NLP

**Definition.** NLP uses metaphor and story as indirect carriers of change (Gordon 1978, *Therapeutic Metaphors*; Erickson's teaching tales). Isomorphic stories map a client's problem into a different setting. (from knowledge)

**Evidence status: [RESEARCH-BACKED] under other names; the NLP-specific "isomorphic" technique is [NLP-CLAIM, UNSUPPORTED].**
- *Narrative transportation*: Green and Brock (2000), "The role of transportation in the persuasiveness of public narratives", *JPSP* 79(5):701-721 ([unofficial mirror](https://gwern.net/docs/www/pdfs.semanticscholar.org/39691cee32e52fbfc33f1a0316aa0d008cb699a4.pdf); verified). Being absorbed in a story (imagery, affect, attention) increases story-consistent beliefs and attitudes. Transported readers detect fewer flaws, so honesty duties apply.
- *Metaphor and reasoning*: Thibodeau and Boroditsky (2011), see 1.8.
- *Self-reference effect*: Rogers, Kuiper and Kirker (1977), "Self-reference and the encoding of personal information", *JPSP* 35(9):677-688, [doi:10.1037/0022-3514.35.9.677](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.35.9.677) (verified; summary): material related to oneself is remembered better. So "you", "your photo", "your birthday" beat "users" or "people". Note boundary condition: effect depends on the material (Maki and McCaul 1985, noted by a secondary summary).

**Application.** A story beats a claim only if it is short, specific and true. One scene (you, the phone, a wrong planet position) is a story. "A journey through the sky of reference" is not. [CRAFT HEURISTIC with RESEARCH-BACKED parts]

# PART 2: APPLICATION TO SIMPLE SOCIAL CONTENT

## 2.1 The core diagnostic: the Meta Model as a "decode cost" test

For every sentence, ask the Meta Model's questions on behalf of a scroller who has 1 second:

1. **Who did it?** (unspecified noun / lost agent / passive)
2. **What exactly did they do?** (unspecified verb; metaphor verb)
3. **To what, exactly?** (missing object; "it"; "the maths")
4. **Compared to what?** (comparative deletion)
5. **Which noun is really a verb?** (nominalisation: "ways to ask", "maths")
6. **Does the reader already hold this referent?** (presupposed "the X")
7. **Is it true of all, always, only?** (universal quantifiers; "only", "kept")

Every question the reader has to answer silently is decode cost. Decode cost is processing disfluency. The evidence that disfluency lowers liking and perceived truth and makes writers seem less capable is solid (Reber et al. 2004; Alter and Oppenheimer 2009; Oppenheimer 2006). [RESEARCH-BACKED] The step from "lab disfluency" to "scroll-past on Instagram" is an inference; I found no controlled social-media test of this. [INFERENCE]

## 2.2 Is the "AI voice" an accidental Milton Model?

**Partly, and in the worst possible way.** [INFERENCE]

Structural match: the failed lines are full of Milton ingredients (nominalisations, unspecified verbs, deleted agents, mind-reading by implication, metaphor). They are "artfully vague" in form.

Why it produces scroll-past rather than trance, in four differences:

1. **Whose blank is it?** A Milton pattern leaves a gap the *listener* fills with *their own experience* ("a time when you felt...") and so can't be wrong. The AI-voice lines leave a gap the *writer* owns: the referent is a specific fact in the writer's head ("moving Saturn" = a particular bug). The reader can't fill it with their own life, so they have to guess the writer's meaning and usually can't.
2. **Cooperative attention.** Hypnosis assumes a seated, motivated, one-to-one listener who stays through the vagueness. A feed gives one second and no obligation. The default response to a vague line in a feed is to skip it.
3. **Perceivable curiosity gap.** Curiosity needs the reader to *know what they don't know* (Loewenstein 1994). "Moving Saturn" doesn't tell a general reader there is anything to be curious about; it's just opaque. Contrast "I asked an AI where Saturn was. It gave me a different answer every time." That makes a clear gap (why?). [INFERENCE from Loewenstein]
4. **Style over content.** AI writes in the cadence of aphoristic marketing copy (short fragments, parallel pairs: "One sky, two ways to count it."). Those shapes carry the *feel* of insight without the content. I could not find research on why LLMs favour this; that explanation is a guess. [INFERENCE]

Also note an **accidental Gricean violation**: readers assume a writer is being relevant and cooperative, and spend effort looking for the intended meaning (Grice 1975; Sperber and Wilson, from knowledge). A line with hidden meaning raises that effort; if no payoff comes, trust drops. [INFERENCE]

## 2.3 Meta Model diagnosis of each failed line

Facts in the rewrites are placeholders for what really happened; I do not know the details of the Panditji app beyond what the task text says, so the rewrites mark where the creator must supply the specific. I infer meanings from context (Saturn, sidereal vs tropical, an ephemeris) and flag them as guesses.

### Line 1: "The AI kept moving Saturn. So I took the maths away from it."
- **Unspecified verb:** "moving" (moved how? In the sky? In the answer? Between answers?). Metaphorical verb.
- **Missing details:** moved *from what to what*? How often? ("kept")
- **Nominalisation / unspecified noun:** "the maths" (which calculation? planet positions? a sign boundary?). "It" (the AI, or the maths?).
- **Presupposed referent:** "the AI", "the maths" (definite article = assumes the reader knows which).
- **Unspecified verb:** "took away".
- **Reader must reconstruct:** the AI gave a different Saturn position each time I asked; I replaced that step with a normal calculation.
- **Plain rewrite (template):** "I asked an AI where Saturn was on my birthday. It gave a different answer every time. So now a small program calculates it, and the AI only writes the explanation."
- **Level:** it is a method sentence (capability level), at the very point where the reader needs a scene.

### Line 2: "One sky, two ways to count it."
- **Metaphor / nominalisation:** "sky" (the sky? the zodiac? the chart?), "ways to count" (counting what?).
- **Deleted:** what is counted (positions? signs? birthdays?), who counts, *and which two ways*. The reader gets a number (two) but no items.
- **Complex equivalence by implication:** "two methods exist" implies "so the answers differ and one is right".
- **Reader must reconstruct:** (probably) Vedic and Western astrology place the same planet in different signs.
- **Plain rewrite (template):** "Western and Vedic astrology put the same planet in different signs. Panditji uses the Vedic one."
- **Curse of knowledge:** insiders find this pair obvious; outsiders have no idea there are "two ways".

### Line 3: "Checked against the sky's reference books."
- **Passive with the agent deleted:** *who* checked? The AI? The phone? You?
- **Unspecified verb:** "checked" (compared numbers? how closely?). **Unspecified noun:** "the sky's reference books" (a metaphor for a table of planet positions; reader cannot decode).
- **Missing result:** checked and found what?
- **Plain rewrite (template):** "I compared its planet positions with [NASA's table / the real source], and they matched to [X]."
- **Evidence note:** the one concrete fact the reader would trust, the comparison result, is the thing deleted. [INFERENCE]

### Line 4: "427 ways to ask the wrong thing. Tested."
- **Nominalisation:** "ways to ask the wrong thing" (ask whom, wrong in what sense?). Contradictory type: you can ask a question wrongly, but "the wrong thing" is a different idea (a wrong subject). Two meanings stacked.
- **Passive fragment with the agent and object deleted:** "Tested." (by whom? what was tested? and the result?).
- **A good element hidden here:** a **specific number** (427). Specifics are credible and concrete, but unanchored specifics read as decoration. The number needs a referent. [CRAFT HEURISTIC]
- **Plain rewrite (template):** "I tried 427 different questions on the app, including some meant to trick it. It got [N] wrong." (Use only if true; supply N.)

### Line 5: "The phone does the maths. The model only explains it."
- **Jargon noun:** "the model" (a general reader hears "fashion model"; a tech reader hears "LLM"). Jargon is a curse-of-knowledge case.
- **Nominalisation:** "maths". **Pronoun:** "it".
- **Universal quantifier:** "only".
- **Metaphor of agency:** a phone "does" maths, a model "explains" (anthropomorphism; fine, but it adds distance).
- **Plain rewrite (template):** "Your phone works out where the planets are. The AI just explains it in plain words."
- **Note:** this is the best of the five; its structure (A does X, B does Y) is already clear. It fails mainly on "model" and "maths".

### Pattern across all five
- The definite article ("the maths", "the model", "the sky's reference books") presupposes a referent the audience does not have (Loftus 1975 logic; 1.6).
- Passives and fragments delete the agent ("Checked", "Tested").
- Nominalisations freeze actions into nouns ("ways to count", "maths", "reference books").
- Metaphors replace plain verbs ("moving Saturn", "took away").
- None names a single picturable thing a reader could see: a phone, a birthday, a chart, a wrong answer.
- Common cause: the writer is in first position and the curse of knowledge hides it from them.

## 2.4 Rules a writer can follow

Each rule gets an evidence tag so the team knows how much weight to give it.

1. **Say who did what to what.** Put a named subject, a plain verb, a concrete object in each sentence. ("I asked an AI where Saturn was.") [RESEARCH-BACKED on fluency/concreteness; the format is INFERENCE]
2. **Use picturable nouns.** "Phone", "birthday", "photo", "chart" beat "model", "maths", "workflow", "pipeline". Concreteness helps memory and processing (Paivio; Packard and Berger). Concrete means checkable fact, not ornament (Lopez and Garza 2022). [RESEARCH-BACKED]
3. **Turn nouns back into verbs.** "Ways to count" -> "count"; "the maths" -> "work out where the planets are". (Reverse nominalisation.) [CRAFT HEURISTIC]
4. **Name the actor in passives.** "I tested it", not "Tested." [CRAFT HEURISTIC]
5. **Never use the definite article for something the reader hasn't met.** Introduce with "a" first, "the" second. "A small program" -> then "the program". (Loftus 1975 on how "the" presupposes.) [RESEARCH-BACKED on presupposition; application INFERENCE]
6. **Pace before you lead.** Open with something the reader already knows or has felt; then add exactly one new idea. Slide 1 = their world; slide 2-3 = the twist. [CRAFT HEURISTIC; consistent with prior-knowledge findings]
7. **Chunk down to one example.** After any claim, show one specific instance: a number, a name, a photo, a before/after. One example beats three abstractions. [RESEARCH-BACKED via construal level and concreteness; INFERENCE for posts]
8. **One idea per slide, one sentence per idea, plain words.** Written for fluency. (Reber 2004; Oppenheimer 2006.) [RESEARCH-BACKED on fluency; "one per slide" is CRAFT HEURISTIC]
9. **Make the reader the subject.** "You", "your photo", "your chart". Self-reference aids memory. [RESEARCH-BACKED, with boundary conditions]
10. **Make the curiosity gap visible.** State the surprising outcome plainly and leave the *why* for later. ("It gave me a different answer every time. Here's why." not "The AI kept moving Saturn.") The reader must be able to see what they don't know. [INFERENCE from Loewenstein 1994]
11. **Define jargon at first use or avoid it.** "Model" -> "the AI". For tech readers, put the jargon behind the "more" tap. [RESEARCH-BACKED on curse of knowledge]
12. **Metaphor only if the source domain is more familiar than the target and you say what it maps to.** "Like a calculator checking a student's homework" is fine; "the sky's reference books" is not. [CRAFT HEURISTIC; metaphors shape reasoning (Thibodeau and Boroditsky), so use them honestly]
13. **Show the check.** If you claim you verified something, state the result. ("Matched NASA's table to 1 arcminute.") Unchecked claims invite scepticism and illusory-truth ethics concerns. [CRAFT HEURISTIC with ethics support]
14. **Tell the truth about limits.** Say what the app calculates versus what is tradition (astrology). This also protects against Barnum-style accusation. [INFERENCE; brand]

## 2.6 Two audiences

- **General audience** (cool things AI can do or build; doesn't care how): needs Meta Model recovery at the *behaviour* level. "I built an app that reads your astrology chart and explains it." Concrete result, picturable example, no mechanism. [INFERENCE]
- **Tech enthusiasts** (will tap "more" if curious): they respond to a *specific, surprising mechanism* but still need the setup stated plainly. Hook: a clear conflict ("The AI got Saturn wrong, differently each time."). Depth later: "so I moved the calculation out of the AI." The mechanism line is only legible after the problem line. [INFERENCE]
- One post can serve both by ordering: slide 1-2 for everyone (what happened, in plain words); slide 3+ for the curious (how). The failed carousel inverted this: method lines first, scene never. [INFERENCE]

## 2.7 Carousel arcs: pacing, leading, future pacing

A generic arc built from the concepts above. Every slide is one sentence. [INFERENCE, assembled from the tagged parts above; untested]

1. **Slide 1, hook (pace + visible gap):** a concrete, relatable scene or result in plain words. ("I asked an AI where Saturn was on my birthday. It said three different things.") No jargon, no metaphors.
2. **Slide 2, pace deeper:** one more fact the reader will recognise or accept. ("Same question, same app, three answers.")
3. **Slide 3, the lead (new idea):** exactly one new thing. ("AI is bad at maths it can't check. So I stopped asking it to do the maths.")
4. **Slide 4, chunk down:** one concrete example or number. ("Now a small program does the calculation. The AI explains the result.")
5. **Slide 5, proof:** the check, with its result. ("I compared 427 chart readings against [source]. [N] differed.")
6. **Slide 6, honesty line:** what is computed, what is tradition, that AI was used. (Brand: disclose.)
7. **Slide 7, CTA / future pace:** one small next step in a concrete situation. ("Next time you wonder what your chart says, try it with your own birth time.") One action only.

Reels and Shorts (spoken): the same arc, but sentences must be even shorter because there is no re-read; the first 3 seconds should name a concrete object or result; on-screen text should repeat the plain line. Audio makes presupposition failures worse (a listener can't glance back). [CRAFT HEURISTIC / INFERENCE]

Captions: the caption can carry the "more" layer for tech readers: the mechanism, the source, the link, plain and in full sentences. [INFERENCE]

## 2.10 Bottom line

Throw away NLP the system; keep the Meta Model as a lint tool, because what it checks (who/what/how/compared-to-what, picturable nouns, shared referents) is what fluency, concreteness, presupposition and curse-of-knowledge research independently support. Pace the reader's real situation, lead with one plain new idea, chunk down to one example, show the check, and end with one small concrete next step. Do not use embedded commands, covert hypnotic patterns, engineered yes-sets or presupposition to smuggle claims, and be especially careful with vague flattering language on an astrology account.

## 3.1 Meta Model audit of cover lines, headlines and hooks

### Method (read this before the numbers)

- **Unit:** one headline, cover line, hook or title, judged from the line alone by a cold reader who has never met the account (the same test the first-time reader applied, per reader_feedback.md).
- **Seven yes/no codes per line**, at most 7 per line:
  - **DA** deleted agent: an event or state with no recoverable doer (passives, "Tested.", nominalized events). Imperatives addressed to the reader are NOT counted.
  - **UV** unspecified or metaphorical verb: the main verb names no action a camera could film ("moving", "does", "checked", "means", "stay in the game"). A line with no verb at all counts as UV.
  - **NOM** nominalization: a process frozen into a noun that carries the claim ("ways to count", "the fall of", "approval", "coping").
  - **PD** presupposed "the X" or insider term the cold reader cannot resolve from the line ("the maths", "Tokenmaxxing", "Claude Code").
  - **UQ** explicit universal or limiting quantifier (every, never, only, no ..., everyone). "Most" is not counted.
  - **UN** unanchored number: a count whose counted thing is unspecified or not picturable ("two ways", "427 ways", "3.8").
  - **MET** metaphor standing where a plain noun or verb exists ("fairy tale", "reference books", "sink").
- **Strict and lenient scores for strong lines.** "Strict" counts only clear violations. "Len" also counts borderline cases (for example "react", "demystify", "see sound", "runs on"). Both are reported so the result does not depend on how I treated edge cases.

### What the counts cannot show (state this plainly to the team)

- **The coder was not blind.** I knew which lines were strong and weak. I coded to written rules and report strict and lenient scores, but bias cannot be ruled out. A second blind coder is the minimum next step.
- **Circular labels.** The harvesters picked weak examples partly because they looked unclear to them, and "unclear" overlaps with my codes (jargon, metaphor, missing agent). The effect is real, but part of the gap may be built into the selection.
- **Engagement is confounded.** The harvest files warn on nearly every account that reach tracks fame, news timing, follower count, sponsorship and offers more than clarity (a text-free Nvidia keynote photo dump got 59.6K likes; the Instagram course offer got 12.6K likes and 3.6K comments from a keyword CTA). - **Line-alone coding is harsh on mid-chain slides.** "One company controls the machines." ## 3.2 Pacing and leading in real arcs

As used here, a **pace** is a line or slide the viewer would nod to because it names their current situation, belief, guess or objection. A **lead** is the one new idea that follows. A **hinge** is the plain word that turns one into the other. [CRAFT HEURISTIC borrowed from NLP. The "start from what the audience already holds" order matches the prior-knowledge and curse-of-knowledge points in Part 1.]

**1. @openai "This legend built an app" (https://www.instagram.com/p/DfubZ5zm2FZ/).**
- Pace: S1 "This legend built an app to help your grandparents not get scammed." (the reader's grandparents and the fear that scammers target them). S2 "His mother-in-law fell for a common scam tactic." (a specific family event).
- Hinge: "Matt decided to make an app to help the elderly learn about known scams out there."
- Lead (one new idea: an ordinary person can build a real tool with ChatGPT in about a week): S3 "Matt worked with ChatGPT to outline an app that could be as accessible as possible." S4 "Matt used the outline and ChatGPT's software recommendations to build the app." S5 "A week later, Scam Skeptic was ready to teach people how to spot and avoid common scams."
- Shape: 2 slides pace, 3 slides lead, no CTA slide. Note "an app" (S3) before "the outline" and "the app" (S4).

**2. Jacklyn Collins, Before/Then/So/Now (https://www.instagram.com/p/DezcWvgoP1Z/).**
- Pace: S2 "Before this, I sold digital products." and "I don't understand a thing about code. Honestly, I still don't. And I had never built an app." This paces the non-coder's belief ("I can't build things") by letting the author hold it too.
- Hinge and lead: S3 "Then I saw what people were building with AI." S4 "So I built a home for them." S5 "Now I get to celebrate them."
- New idea: non-coders build apps with AI. The four headlines read as one sentence.

**3. Cleo Abram, "Guess How Far The Sun Is" (https://www.youtube.com/shorts/DcAjv8YRKN8).**
- Pace: 0:00 "Most people don't understand just how far away the sun really is. If you lined up all of the planets in our solar system, you'd make it only from Earth to the moon." Then 0:08 the viewer is made to state their own belief: "Remember your guess. Don't change it." This is the clearest explicit pace in the corpus: the viewer's map is committed before it is challenged.
- Lead: 0:13 "95 times = Venus, 192 = Mercury, you got to keep going"; 0:23 the basketball, pin and court picture; 0:31 payoff "374".
- Close: "But what if you dove into the sun? To see that. Subscribe."

**4. Cleo Abram, "You're dead. What next?" (https://www.instagram.com/reel/DfKroAgIzjF/).**
- Pace: 0.2s "SO YOU'RE DEAD..." 1.1s "MY CONDOLENCES." (a shared hypothetical, with a joke that sets a human tone).
- Lead: 3s "FOR YOU TO GO FROM THIS", then stage labels, for example 20s the card "DAYS 0-3 / 1. AUTOLYSIS" with the speech explaining the one jargon word. The new idea: a stage-by-stage answer to "what happens next".

**5. Finshots, "What a bad day for Nvidia means to you" (https://www.instagram.com/p/Dfrk61HlAa2/).**
- Pace: S2 scale ("every company listed on the BSE put together is worth about Rs.511 lakh crore"). S3 "Here's the thing. You may have felt this fall without ever buying a single Nvidia share." This starts from what the reader believes (I don't own Nvidia) and then leads.
- Pace of an objection: S7 "'But I bought 100 companies, not one.' You did and that's the catch." The corpus's best case of quoting the viewer's likely objection verbatim, then answering it.
- Lead: S4 "An index fund buys a bit of every company on a list" (the basket); S5 "Out of every Rs.100 in an S&P 500 fund, about Rs.8.50 goes to Nvidia."; S6 "That wipes out about 88 paise from every Rs.100 in your fund."

**6. School of Life, "The Most Successful Person You Know Is Just as Anxious as You Are" (https://www.instagram.com/p/Dfq8CMOl8Ee/).**
- Pace (twice): S2 "Most of what makes us anxious on a given day looks specific: a party, an email, a flight." and S4 "We tend to fantasise that calm is waiting somewhere specific, a beach eleven hours away, a perfectly decluttered home, a certain amount of money or status."

- Pattern: the reader's own belief, one correcting sentence, then the new idea.

**7. Tiff In Tech, chips (https://www.instagram.com/p/DYkM7zZlAwz/).**

- Lead: each demo is one request and one result ("Yo, Bluey, I need you to point to...").

### The general pattern

1. **A pace the viewer can verify from their own life** in the first one or two units: a grandparent and a scam (O1), "I don't understand a thing about code" (Jacklyn), a guess committed aloud (Cleo), not owning Nvidia (Finshots), a party, an email, a flight (School of Life), the phone in your hand (Tiff).
2. **One plain hinge:** "So", "Then", "But", "Here's the thing", "None of these actually work", "Now bring it home", "Remember your guess". No poetic bridges appear anywhere in the corpus; the harvests say so for the news pages, the creator carousels and the Shorts. [OBSERVED]
3. **One new idea**, not three. Every arc above can be said back in one sentence.
4. **One proof per slide** (a number with its noun, a screenshot, a picture).
5. **Proportion varies.** Stories run about 1 pace to 2-3 lead units. News and list posts (Evolving.ai's 20-slide reaction list, Morning Brew's Nvidia timeline) have almost no lead, because the novelty is in the facts. So pace-then-lead is [CORPUS-CONFIRMED] for explainer, builder and idea posts, and simply not needed for plain news lists.

**Counter-pattern in weak posts and in Tangent's failed carousel.** Weak posts open in the writer's world: "The days of Tokenmaxxing might be over, y'all." (https://www.instagram.com/p/DfsDz6IlfCP/, a term a general reader never used), "Introducing Gemini 3.8 Live ...", "Fugitive imagination". The failed carousel's slide 1, "The AI kept moving Saturn. So I took the maths away from it.", is a lead with no pace: it states the fix before the reader has seen the problem, in terms only the writer has used. The first-time reader said the same thing in her own words: the cover must say exactly what the post is about. [INFERENCE]

Method: on the same 31 strong, 15 weak and 5 failed lines I counted (a) whether the main verb names an action or perception a camera could film (build, replace, resign, call, text, see, speak, reach), and (b) whether the line has a subject or object you could photograph (a person, app, plane, camera, chip, phone). The counts are mine and subjective at the edges.

| Measure | Strong (31) | Weak (15) | Failed (5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main verb names a filmable action or perception | 16 (52%) | 1 (7%) | 0 (0%) |
| Subject or object you could photograph | 23 (74%) | 5 (33%) | 2 (40%) |

One-sided Fisher exact: verbs p about 0.003, picturable nouns p about 0.01. The limits in 3.1 apply (non-blind coding, small n, non-random sample).

Examples [OBSERVED]:
- Doing verbs in strong lines: "built" (O1, Jacklyn, Riley, Ayesha), "replaces" (Axios), "resigned" (Varun), "uncover" (TheAIField T1), "Call instead of text" (Aashish), "go faster than sound" (Cleo plane), "reached ... signatures" (airesearches A1).
- Seeing verbs are rare. Visible perceptual hooks: "This camera allows you to see sound in real time" (MKBHD), "Look at this." (Cleo Antarctica Short, https://www.youtube.com/shorts/ZUirpasicHQ), "Guess how far". Hearing and feeling verbs are almost absent from hooks.
- Verbs in weak lines are abstract: "might be over", "might be in trouble", "Use ... as a Sparring Partner", "Graduate From", "Unlock", "coping with", "has a shelf life". In the failed lines: "kept moving", "took away", "count", "checked", "does", "explains", "Tested".

**What this says about VAK.** The representational-systems doctrine (a preferred visual, auditory or kinaesthetic system, matched by predicates) appears in none of these hooks as a design principle; no account tailors seeing, hearing or feeling words to an audience type. What the corpus supports is the narrower, mainstream idea from Part 1: concrete, picturable subjects and filmable verbs (concreteness effect, dual coding). So matching VAK predicates is [NOT SEEN IN CORPUS] (and [NLP-CLAIM, UNSUPPORTED] per Part 1); concrete verbs and picturable nouns are [CORPUS-CONFIRMED].

**Part 1's caution about "sensory" language holds here.** The Amazon-review finding that sensory reviews were rated less helpful (Lopez and Garza 2022, section 1.4) has a corpus echo: the strongest lines are factual and checkable ("$800", "64 microphones", "88 paise from every Rs.100", "A week later"), not decorative. ## 3.7 Tone: why "It reads your day, in plain words." sounds like a deck

The first-time reader called the copy "very presentation-ish" (reader_feedback.md, point 7). The launch carousel (https://www.instagram.com/p/DfqGtnMkzel/) has these slide headlines, verbatim:
- "It reads your day, in plain words."
- "It finds good hours to begin, and ones to avoid."
- "It answers your questions, from your own chart."
- "Vedic or Western, it reads your chart your way."
- "It matches two charts for marriage, verdict first."
- "It won't tell you when you'll die. Or which coin will 10x."
- "The AI isn't allowed to guess."
- "Free. No app store needed."
- "Small AI apps for the small problems of everyday life."

**Meta Model reading.**
- Seven of nine lines run on "It" or "The AI": an unspecified noun as the subject and no human anywhere. The doer is a product, so there is no person for the reader to meet.
- Habitual present-tense verbs ("reads", "finds", "answers", "matches") state what the product always does: a generalization with no instance (nobody asks anything in these lines).
- "isn't allowed to guess" is a lost performative: allowed by whom? The line hides that this was the author's own design decision.
- The parallel pattern "It [verb]s X, [adverbial]" repeated down the slides is a spec sheet. A spec sheet is read from outside.
- "Small AI apps for the small problems of everyday life" is nominalized throughout (apps, problems, life).
- Two lines pass the same codes: "It won't tell you when you'll die. Or which coin will 10x." (specific, surprising, picturable) and "Free. No app store needed." (concrete). So the deck feel comes from the repeated pattern and the missing person, not from every line.

**Rapport, matching and perceptual positions (Part 1 sections 1.5 and 1.14).**
- NLP says rapport comes from matching the other person's map. The technique claims are unsupported (Part 1), so this is a craft reading: people respond to being addressed by a person. [CRAFT HEURISTIC]
- **Perceptual position.** A third-person "It reads..." line is written from third position: a neutral observer describing a product. A first-person "I built X" line puts the viewer in second position, facing a person whose experience they can imagine. A second-person "you" line puts the viewer in first position, inside their own life. The strong posts occupy first and second position; the deck lives in third. [INFERENCE]

**Corpus evidence for the voice difference.**
- First person, specific, a little self-deprecating: "Hi, I'm Jacklyn. I built Shelf by JC with AI."; "I don't understand a thing about code. Honestly, I still don't." (Jacklyn); "[claude gaslit me multiple times btw so keep prompts clear]" (Ayesha, https://www.instagram.com/p/DZMxVIiCD7L/); "I make API documentation for a living. I know. Stay with me." (Ana Robakidze, https://www.instagram.com/p/DcOQqXRFyTo/); "so, of course, I got one" and "Pulling that trigger is hilarious" (MKBHD); "my condolences" (Cleo); "AI beats me at grunt work. But I beat AI at opinions, jokes, and knowing my audience." (Rowan Cheung). The creator harvest calls the self-deprecating asides "the warmest lines in the set" (lesson J).
- Second person with concrete things: DeepMind D4 (https://www.instagram.com/p/DecN711mjpI/) is also a feature list, yet it is the clearest Google carousel and the most liked (51.5K): "Create 3D games - Turn photos of your desk into a playable 3D racing game using Google Antigravity", "Illustrate complex topics - Turn dense research papers into animated storybooks in the Gemini app". Imperative verbs, "your", a real object. By contrast D3's noun-label cards ("Improved Photorealism - Built on Gemini Omni to better simulate how spaces actually look, feel, and behave.") got 3.3K (launch and brand confounds noted in the harvest), and Nvidia N1 slides 2-3 in corporate voice ("SimBioSys uses NVIDIA technology to power its FDA-cleared TumorSight Risk.") scored clarity 4 in the harvest.
- Third person works when the subject is a named person doing a past-tense, filmable thing: "Matt decided to make an app...", "Trump replaces...", "This legend built...". So the problem is not third person as such. It is "It / The AI", habitual present tense, and no person. [OBSERVED / INFERENCE]

**Rewrite pattern (templates; the creator must confirm each is literally true of Panditji before use):**
- "It reads your day, in plain words." -> "I type my birth time and get my day in plain words." (first person, a doing verb)
- "It answers your questions, from your own chart." -> "Ask it 'is this a good week to sign my lease?' and it answers from your chart." (you, a real question)
- "The AI isn't allowed to guess." -> "I don't let the AI guess. A normal program works out the planet positions. The AI only explains them." (names the doer, fixes the lost performative)
- Keep "It won't tell you when you'll die. Or which coin will 10x." as is, or in first person: "I made it refuse two questions: when you'll die, and which coin will 10x."

**The reader's other advice, mapped to the corpus:** one anchor line per slide that survives a skim, and very few simple words on the cover. The corpus agrees: bold-lead patterns (D1, D4, O1), Tiff's headlines that read as the whole story in order, Finshots' plain bridges. Her request to try a witty and a direct tone has precedent in Cleo's "my condolences" and Ayesha's asides. [CORPUS-CONFIRMED]

## 3.9 Where Milton-style vagueness works in the corpus (and why this supports 2.2)

Part 2 section 2.2 argued that Tangent's lines are vague in the Milton way but fail because the blank belongs to the writer, not the reader. The corpus gives a test of that. The four aphorism posts all score 2-3 violations yet perform:
- "you are only as free as the approval you don't need" (https://www.instagram.com/p/DdVKYsgEdtq/): 2.3K likes, 156 shares, harvest clarity 9/10; the image (a lone silhouette under a looming crowd) supplies the picture.
- "No position can be permanently occupied." (https://www.instagram.com/p/DdHCilmEcsm/): a tiny figure on a tall ladder; 1.1K likes.
- "Just stay in the game" (https://www.instagram.com/p/Dfik-rvTfuJ/): handwritten, a stock idiom; 32K likes.
- "Your relationships will never be better than your self-esteem" (https://www.instagram.com/p/DfsIKa9qBit/): 2.6K likes.

Their blanks are reader-owned: any reader fills "approval", "position" or "the game" with their own life, and each line parses in a second. They sit on accounts whose followers chose them for that mood, and each is paired with a picture or a plain caption that decodes it ("Distraction, Focus"; the caption IS the sentence). The same vagueness without a decoding picture or claim lost: VV5 "Artificial Super Intelligence" got 243 likes and the comment "Hand-shaped lamp". The harvest's own conclusion: "when the caption names a topic instead of stating a claim, the image alone has to do all the work." [OBSERVED / INFERENCE]

So vague works when (1) the gap is the reader's, (2) the line parses in about a second (a stock idiom, short), (3) a picture or plain caption anchors it, and (4) the audience already opted in. Tangent's failed lines meet none of these: the gap is the writer's ("moving Saturn"); the phrases are coined, not stock; the pictures were Western/Vedic sign diagrams; the audience is new. That supports 2.2. It also says proverbs are NOT a good fit for Tangent's two audiences right now (strangers), and are risky on an astrology account (Barnum).
